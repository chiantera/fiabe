Prologue · 4 minutes to read aloud

Prologue

Tilde's stories — to be read before all the others

I told you the firefly's stories because I saw them with my own eyes. I was there at the window that night, and I turned off the lamp myself, and I was the first one to see the meadow light up.

This one I'll tell you differently.

This one I didn't see. Somebody told it to me, many years later, one evening when I was already grown up — and I believed them, on their word, the way you always do, in the end, with the stories that really matter. You'll see, when we get there, that this is exactly what happens inside the story too.

But first there's something I have to tell you, and this time I've never told it before.

Do you remember the house at the top of the hill? The one with the yellow window, the one I watched the meadow light up gold from? It was a good house. The people who lived in it cared about things, and they wouldn't have hurt anybody on purpose, or even by accident, if only they'd known how.

But when they built it, they also dug a well.

And nobody up there ever wondered where the water goes when you move it to dig a well. That water ended up right under the meadow, in a place where somebody had lived their whole life, and it changed course without asking anybody's permission.

In the stories I've already told you, that house was the light in the window: the wonder, the start of a friendship. In these ones it's something else. It's the same house, with the same good people inside it. Only seen from underneath, that house did real harm — and nobody up top knew for a very long time, and by the time they found out, it was too late to put it fully right.

I wanted to tell you this before, not after, because it's an important thing to know from early on: good people can do real harm. Not because they're bad. Because they don't know the meadow has an underneath too.

Under that meadow, in those same years, through one very long night beneath the grass, somebody else was living.

She had no wings, and she made no light, and nobody up above knew her name.

But she held a whole world together, one corridor at a time, and I want to tell you this story exactly the way it was told to me: on somebody's word.

It starts like this:

In the meadow at the foot of the hill, under the grass, there lived a mole called Tilde.

I · 7 minutes to read aloud

The mole who couldn't find her front door

A bedtime story — Book One

In the meadow at the foot of the hill, under the grass, there lived a mole called Tilde.

Tilde was old, her coat was like worn velvet, and everybody in the meadow was fond of her.

She also had a pair of spectacles — a present from the fireflies, who were quite certain they'd help — and she wore them with great pride, even though, let's be honest, moles can't see much even with spectacles on: they changed nothing at all, but they suited her. Her legs creaked a little when it was cold. And now and then, halfway through a thought, she'd stop, look around, and start again with a different one.

But everybody, absolutely everybody, knew one other thing about her:

that Tilde could never find her front door.

She would come out in the evening for a bit of air, take four steps, turn round, and find herself looking at three tufts of grass that were exactly the same.

«Was it this one?»

It was not that one.

«Then it was that one.»

Nor that.

It always ended with somebody walking her home: the ants, or a snail, or a firefly passing by who made her some light. And Tilde would thank them, and slip underground, and after that not a sound came up from down there.

The fireflies, among themselves, said she was a bit scatty. They said it fondly, but they said it.

Then came the night of the storm.

It rained for hours, hard, and when it stopped the meadow was full of puddles, and at one spot near the hedge the ground had opened: a narrow deep crack, with crumbling edges.

A young hedgehog had fallen into it.

He was crying very quietly, because nothing else would come out.

Everybody came. The fireflies gathered over the crack and made light, all of them together, as hard as they could.

And for the first time, the light was no use at all.

You could see the hedgehog perfectly well down there. That was precisely the problem: you could see him, and there was nothing to be done. The crack was too narrow to fly into and too deep to reach, and every time somebody leaned over the edge another piece of earth slid down.

Nobody knew what to do.

Then a firefly said: «Fetch the mole.»

And somebody answered: «Tilde? Tilde can't even find her own house.»

They fetched her anyway.

Tilde came trotting up, listened, and set off decisively in the wrong direction.

There was a very awkward silence.

Then Tilde stopped. She lowered her nose, rested it on the ground, and stayed like that.

And she stopped being funny.

«Ah,» she said. «We're above the root of the oak. So the crack is over that way.»

And she began to dig.

She was gone in three seconds, and from then on the story happened in a place where nobody from the meadow had ever been.

Because under the meadow it isn't dark: under the meadow the dark has nothing to do with anything.

Under the meadow there are roads. There are the corridors Tilde has dug all her life, and she knows them one by one, and she knows where they go the way you know where your own room is with your eyes shut. There's the smell of wet earth, which changes every two steps and tells her everything: clay this way, sand that way, a big stone above there, water running below.

And there are the roots. Tilde knows them by name: the oak's root is thick and knotted, the hedge's is thin and dense as hair, and if you follow that one you come to the crack.

Tilde followed that one.

She came up beside the hedgehog from underneath, where the earth wasn't crumbling. She dug a wide, comfortable slope, taking her time, because a thing done in a hurry falls in.

And then she said: «Come on, walk slowly. Put your feet where I put mine.»

Up above, in the meadow, they were all waiting round the crack.

But the hedgehog didn't come out of the crack.

He came out of a new hole, three metres further on, in the middle of the grass, covered in earth and alive.

And Tilde came out behind him.

«But how did you do it?» asked a firefly.

Tilde thought about it, as though she had never considered it before.

«From underneath it's all different,» she said. «From underneath I never get lost.»

Then she said goodnight to everybody, took four steps, turned round, and found herself looking at three tufts of grass that were exactly the same.

«Was it this one?»

It was not that one.

And so two fireflies walked her home, making light for her, and neither of them said she was scatty.

There's always somewhere you're very good indeed, my love.

It may not be the place where everybody is watching.

And if there's a place where you always get lost, that means nothing at all: it only means that isn't the place where you know your way.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

II · 7 minutes to read aloud

The roads under the meadow

A bedtime story — Book Two, following “The mole who couldn't find her front door”

Early one morning, while Tilde was doing the rounds of her corridors, she heard something that had never happened to her before.

She heard somebody running.

Not just the sound of feet: an actual run, back and forth, flat out, in the long corridor that goes under the hedge.

Tilde went to look.

It was a shrew.

He was tiny and grey, with a pointed nose, and he was running from one end to the other like a marble in a box.

«Good morning,» said Tilde.

The shrew stopped dead.

«Oh help,» he said. «Oh help oh help oh help. I fell in. There was a hole, I was going past, I fell in, I tried to climb out, you can't climb out, I tried that way, that way's no good, I tried this way and I think I've been this way already, and anyway I need to eat.»

«You need to eat?»

«Every twenty minutes,» said the shrew. «If I don't eat every twenty minutes I die. It's a thing about me.»

«Ah,» said Tilde. «Then we'd better get on.»

And off she went.

The shrew ran after her and started talking, and practically never stopped.

His name was Pino.

They were out in under a minute: you only had to turn left and then go up.

Pino set about eating, which is to say he vanished into a tuft of grass and made a noise of things snapping, and then popped out again very pleased and said:

«But how did you do that?»

«Do what?»

«Know which way to go! They were all the same!»

Tilde thought about it.

«They weren't the same,» she said.

«But can you... see?» asked Pino, who had noticed the spectacles by now.

«Hardly at all,» said Tilde. «Even with these, if it comes to that. But you don't need to see, to get about down here. You need to feel.»

And Pino, who was rude out of curiosity rather than unkindness, said: «Will you show me?»

So Tilde did something she had never done before: she showed somebody round her house.

They went down.

«This is the upper round,» said Tilde. «It's for checking. I go along it twice a day.»

«What for?»

«Because something is always falling into the corridors. A little worm, a seed, something that's taken a wrong turn. If you don't go round, you don't find anything.»

Then they went down further.

«Down here nothing falls in, and that's why it's quieter.»

Pino touched the wall. «It's smooth!»

«I made that one forty years ago,» said Tilde. «The more you go along it, the smoother it gets. You can tell how old a road is from its walls.»

They went on, and at a certain point Tilde stopped and said: «Be quiet a moment.»

With enormous effort, Pino was quiet.

And then he heard it.

A long low sound, coming from inside the earth, like putting your ear to a shell.

«What's that?»

«The brook,» said Tilde. «It runs on the other side of that wall. When the wall makes that noise it means you're under the willow, and if you're under the willow your house is to the right.»

Pino's mouth fell open.

Then they visited the last room, which was round, right at the bottom of everything, with soft walls of dry grass. That was where Tilde slept.

«This is lovely,» said Pino, speaking quietly for the first time.

And then he asked again: «But how do you remember it all?»

Tilde opened her mouth to say «I don't know», which was the answer she always gave.

But that time she stopped, and actually thought about it.

«It isn't that I remember it with my head,» she said at last. «I remember it with my feet.»

«Eh?»

«The earth changes at every step. Over there it's clay and it sticks. Over here it's sand and it slides. Under the oak it smells of mushrooms. Near the brook it's cold and heavy. Past the hedge there's a stretch full of little stones that hurt.» Tilde shrugged. «I don't decide where to go. I feel where I am.»

Pino thought about that for the whole three seconds he was able to keep still.

«Then it's easy!»

«For me it is,» said Tilde. «You try shutting your eyes in your bedroom and walking to the door.»

«I can do that!»

«There you are,» said Tilde. «Same thing. Only my house is a bit bigger.»

Then she saw him out, because twenty minutes had gone by.

There are things you can do perfectly well and couldn't explain to anybody, my love.

It doesn't mean you don't know them: it means you know them so well that you've put them away, the way you put a thing in a drawer that opens by itself when you need it.

Have a think: how many steps is it from your bed to the door?

You've no idea. But in the dark you never get it wrong.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

III · 5 minutes to read aloud

Seven

A bedtime story — Book Three, following “The roads under the meadow”

Under the meadow, in a cell about this big, attached to a thin root, there was a creature doing nothing.

She wasn't asleep. She wasn't ill. She wasn't sad.

She was simply doing nothing, and she had been doing it for seven years.

Her name was Seven, and she was a cicada nymph.

Tilde had always known her. She had found her when she was the size of a grain of rice, the year she redid the east corridor, and ever since she looked in now and then to see how things were going.

«How's it going?»

«Fine,» Seven would say.

And that was that.

Tilde always sat down for a moment before leaving, because getting up quickly, these days, cost her a small «ouch» in her back legs.

One day Tilde took Pino down, who the moment he heard about the cell wanted to see it at once, and who, faced with Seven, stayed quiet for nearly two whole seconds.

Then he said: «What do you do?»

«I stay here,» said Seven.

«And then?»

«And then I stay here.»

Pino looked at Tilde, looked at the cell, looked at Seven again.

«For how long?»

«Seven years.»

Pino made a noise as though something had dropped inside his stomach.

«Seven years? What have you done in seven years?»

«This,» said Seven.

«But don't you get bored?»

And here Seven moved for the first time, only slightly, the way a person turns over in bed.

«No,» she said. «Because I'm not just sitting still. I'm becoming.»

«Becoming what?»

«I don't know yet. Something with wings.»

Pino, who in seven years would be born, grow up, grow old and die four times over, simply could not take it in.

«But it's wasted time!»

«Touch my back,» said Seven.

Pino put out a paw and touched.

«What's this hard stuff?»

«That wasn't there last year,» said Seven.

On the way out, Pino was unusually silent.

«It doesn't add up,» he said to Tilde. «Somebody sits still for seven years and something happens anyway?»

«Look around you,» said Tilde.

«I can't see anything, it's dark.»

«Exactly. Touch.»

Pino touched the corridor wall.

«That's a root,» said Tilde. «When I dug this stretch, that root wasn't there. Now it's as thick as my arm. I've never seen it move. Nobody has ever seen it move.»

They went on in the dark.

«The oak up there is fifty times your height,» said Tilde. «It came out of an acorn. Nobody ever watched it grow, not for a single afternoon. And yet there it is.»

Pino considered this, which cost him considerable physical effort.

«So the long things can't be seen while they're happening.»

«There you are,» said Tilde. «Good.»

That night Pino couldn't sleep, which for him meant staying awake twenty-two minutes together.

And every so often, in the years that followed, he would go back down to the cell on his own and sit outside it and ask: «How's it going?»

And Seven would say: «Fine.»

And he would stay a moment, and then dash off to eat.

There are things that take an enormously long time, my love.

Learning to read. Getting tall. Stopping being frightened of something.

And because they take so long, while they're happening it looks as though nothing is happening at all, and you want to say I can't do it or this is wasted time.

It isn't wasted time. It's just long.

Tonight as well, while you're asleep and doing absolutely nothing, you are becoming.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

IV · 5 minutes to read aloud

The neighbour upstairs

A bedtime story — Book Four, following “Seven”

There was one thing Tilde liked more than anything else, and she had never told anybody because she didn't know how you'd say it.

She liked it when the ceiling trembled.

It happened in summer, every evening, as soon as it went dark up above. It would start quietly, then settle, and carry on for hours: frin frin, frin frin, always the same, never identical.

For the meadow up there, that was a song.

For Tilde it wasn't a sound. Tilde has ears, but underground sounds don't amount to much: what really reaches you is the way the earth shakes. And on those evenings the earth shook in a tiny, regular way, like somebody tapping two fingers on your back to tell you they're there.

It came to her through her feet. It came through her nose, if she rested it on the floor.

Tilde had grown up with that trembling against her. She had dug half her corridors with that frin frin in the ceiling over her head.

She had never known who it was.

One night in July she decided to find out.

She went up to the place where the ceiling trembled most, which was under the big root of the oak, and there she stopped. It had cost her a little more effort than it used to: her legs complained more often these days. Then she lifted a paw and knocked. Three little knocks.

Above, the frin frin stopped dead.

Tilde waited.

And after a moment, from the ceiling, came three knocks back.

Tilde stood there in the dark with her heart going, which at her age wasn't something that happened often.

Then she knocked again: two knocks.

Upstairs, two knocks.

They went on like that for half an hour, without either of them knowing who the other was and without either wondering about it too much, and then the frin frin started up again, and Tilde went back down as pleased as a child.

They actually met at the end of that summer, when she came up by mistake two steps from the root and he was out there.

He was an old cricket. His name was Rocco.

«Ah,» said Rocco. «You're the one who knocks.»

«You're the one who sings.»

«Sings?» Rocco seemed surprised. «You can hear me singing? From down there?»

«I can't hear you,» said Tilde. «I touch you.»

And since Rocco didn't understand, Tilde explained: that for her the song had never been a sound but something that came up through the earth into her feet; that she knew it by its rhythm and not by its voice; and that when he started in the evening, she knew it was time to stop working.

Rocco was quiet for a good while.

Then he said: «I sing so the ones above can hear me.»

«I know.»

«It never occurred to me it went down as well.»

«Things go downwards too,» said Tilde. «Nobody ever thinks about it.»

From that evening they had a habit, which is the best thing two who hardly ever see each other can have.

In the evening, when Rocco began, Tilde knocked three times on the ceiling: I'm here.

And Rocco, without stopping, left a tiny gap, a hole in the rhythm, which meant: me too.

Nobody in the meadow ever noticed. The fireflies said the cricket dropped a beat on certain evenings.

There's something you do that reaches somebody you don't know about, my love.

From a direction you'd never imagine, to somebody you never sent it to.

You'll almost never find out. But it happens every single time, and every so often one of them knocks to tell you.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

V · 6 minutes to read aloud

The water

A bedtime story — Book Five, following “The neighbour upstairs”

It started with nothing at all.

Tilde was doing the upper round one afternoon in June, and when she reached the west corridor she put a paw down and drew it back.

It was damp.

Not wet: damp. Like a cellar wall.

Tilde stood there for quite a while.

It had happened to her before, lately: feeling a thing in her joints a few days before feeling it in the corridors — a dull little ache, right in her front legs, that the old moles of the meadow called «the barometer» among themselves. That morning the barometer had been right.

That corridor had been dry for twenty years. She had dug it herself in good pale earth that didn't hold water, and in twenty years nothing had ever happened there, not even after the big storms.

She ran her paw along the whole wall. Damp for four paces, then dry.

All right, she thought. A root has come through.

The following week it was damp for ten paces.

Tilde stopped doing the upper round the way she always did and began doing it another way: slowly, nose to the floor, stopping now and then to lick a finger and touch the wall and feel where the cold was pulling.

And she found that the damp wasn't a spot. It was a stripe. It came from the north — from the hill side — and ran down towards the brook across half the meadow, like the trail of a snail a hundred metres long.

And it was moving.

Every week it was a little further over.

Tilde didn't know what it was, but she knew perfectly well what it meant: the water under the meadow had changed course.

So she went up to say so.

Up above it was the finest summer anybody could remember.

The grass was tall, greener than it had ever been, full of flowers even in the places where there were usually hardly any. The ants said it was a year to tell your grandchildren about. The snails were fat and happy.

«The water is moving,» said Tilde.

«The water?» said the ants. «It hasn't rained for three weeks.»

«Underneath.»

The ants looked at one another.

«Tilde,» said one of them kindly, «look at this grass.»

And it was true, and that was exactly the problem: the grass was beautiful *because* the water was moving there. The meadow was drinking somebody else's luck without knowing it.

But that was the part Tilde couldn't get across — or rather: she said it, and nobody understood.

«And then what happens?» asked a snail.

«I don't know yet.»

«Then let's wait and see,» said the snail, very reasonably.

Tilde went back down.

Pino found her sitting in the west corridor, still, with her nose against the damp wall.

«What are you doing?»

«Listening.»

Pino put his ear to it as well. «I can't hear anything.»

«Exactly.»

«What am I supposed to hear?»

«Behind there, ten paces along, is the seam of water that used to run under the willow.» Tilde tapped twice with her paw. «I've heard it since I was born. It's the background noise of this house.»

Pino listened harder.

«It's gone?»

«It's further away,» said Tilde. «By quite a bit.»

They were quiet a moment.

«But if everything's fine up above,» said Pino, «maybe it isn't serious.»

«Maybe,» said Tilde.

And that night, for the first time in her life, she went to sleep in a different room: the highest one, nearest the way out.

She didn't tell anybody, and she didn't explain it to herself either. It simply seemed like a good idea.

There are times when you notice something before everybody else, my love.

And when that happens hardly anyone believes you, because they look out of the window and outside there's sunshine.

It doesn't mean you're wrong. It only means you were looking from somewhere else, and from there you see it sooner.

Tell them anyway. And in the meantime, if it feels right, sleep wherever you sleep best.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet — almost everything — the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

VI · 7 minutes to read aloud

The ones up there

A bedtime story — Book Six, following “The water”

In the end Tilde decided to go and see.

Not up: down. She wanted to follow the damp stripe back to where it started, and it started to the north, on the hill side, where she had never been because there had never been anything to do there.

She prepared for the journey the way you prepare for serious journeys, which is by eating a great deal and telling nobody.

Pino found out anyway and insisted on coming, and after two hours he said he was hungry and went back, and Tilde carried on alone.

It took her six days.

Not because it was far, but because digging uphill is another business entirely: the earth comes down at you instead of going away, and you have to stop often, and a sloping tunnel dug in a hurry falls in.

So she went: a stretch, a rest, a stretch, a rest — partly because that's how a climb is, partly because her legs, these days, asked for a rest even before she'd asked them for one. And at every stop she put her nose to the wall and checked she was still on the stripe.

The earth changed as she went.

First it got stonier. Then colder. Then, on the fourth day, it changed taste altogether: it tasted of iron and of things stirred about, like a badly made cake.

Tilde stopped, because that was earth somebody had taken out and put back.

It hadn't been a mole. A mole packs the earth behind her and leaves it firm. This had been tipped in, in big lumps, with stones in it that had no business being there and a piece of something hard and smooth that wasn't a stone.

On the fifth day she found the wall.

Not a wall of hard earth: a real wall, straight and cold, going down and down and never ending. Tilde walked along beside it for a long way, touching it, and understood she was going round something enormous.

It was the new house, from below. The foundations.

And on the sixth day, following the cold, she found the thing.

It was a pipe. Round, smooth, as wide as she was, and it went straight down, far further down than Tilde had ever been in her life: with her nose against it she could feel air rising from below, and with the air a sound of water, very distant and very deep.

Tilde stayed there a long time, working it out.

Then she worked it out, and working it out was no satisfaction at all.

The water under the meadow hadn't gone off on its own. The ones up there had made a hole to go and fetch it, and water, which always goes wherever the way is easiest, had stopped running under the willow and now came here.

And then, of course, it had to come out somewhere.

And it was coming out in the middle of the meadow, slowly, spreading a little wider every week.

Tilde started back.

And on the way back the worst part of the whole business came to her, the part that hurt more than the water did: there was nobody to be angry with.

If it had been a storm, it was the storm. If it had been a badger caving in her corridors, it was the badger.

But up there they didn't even know the meadow had an underneath.

They had made a hole to drink from, which is the most ordinary thing in the world, and they hadn't the faintest idea they had moved a river through somebody's ceiling.

They weren't wicked. They were only big, and distracted, and above.

That same summer, up there at the top of the hill, a window stayed lit all night, and an old firefly set off from the meadow to go and see what it was.

Tilde never knew, and knowing would have done her no good at all.

She got home on the ninth day, more tired than she had ever been, crawled into the highest room and slept for two whole days.

Then she got up, and began to think about where to move house.

Sometimes it's nobody's fault, my love.

That's the hardest thing of all to swallow, much harder than when somebody is to blame: because if there's somebody to blame you can at least be angry, and being angry is comfortable.

Instead, sometimes a thing has simply happened, and all that's left is to say: all right. And then to see what can be done.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, the roads are almost all where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

VII · 6 minutes to read aloud

The room that isn't there any more

A bedtime story — Book Seven, following “The ones up there”

The water came in October, with the first serious rain.

It didn't come in the way a burglar comes in. It came in the way something comes in that has every right to be passing through.

Tilde felt it from her bed: a distant hiss, then the sound of water running somewhere it had never run, and under her feet a floor beginning to be cold in a new way.

She got up and went to look.

The low corridors, the old ones, the ones she had dug when she was young, were filling up.

There was nothing to be done, and Tilde understood that in the first ten seconds. You can't plug water that comes from below: block it here and it comes out there, and meanwhile everything else goes soft, and a house with soft walls isn't a house any more, it's a collapse that's waiting.

They had to be closed.

Closing means exactly that: filling the corridors with packed earth, one by one, so the water stays on its side and doesn't come and drink the rooms above.

Tilde sat down for a moment, because she needed a moment.

Then she said: «All right.»

And before she started, she did something that wasn't necessary.

She went down.

She went down into the cold water, swimming — moles swim, badly, but they swim, and with her arthritis it hurt rather more than usual — and went to see the old rooms one last time.

She went through the low chamber with the round ceiling, the first she had ever made on her own: it had taken her a whole summer and it had come out crooked, and you could still see where she had got the curve wrong.

She went through the long corridor, the one where the wall is so smooth it's like glass, polished by forty years of her going along it.

She went past the place with the big stone, the one she had spent three days trying to shift when she was young before deciding to go round it. The stone was still there. It seemed silly now to have got so cross about it.

And she went through the alcove where she used to keep the good things, which had been empty for years and which she still called the larder.

Then she came back up, and started closing.

It took her eleven days.

Pino came every day and carried earth, which is a thing a shrew does extremely badly, but he came.

On the tenth day, while they were closing the long corridor, Pino said: «Doesn't it make you sad?»

«Of course it makes me sad.»

«Then why aren't you crying?»

Tilde pushed another pawful of earth.

«Because I'm working,» she said. «I'll cry afterwards.»

On the eleventh day they finished.

Tilde's house was now half of what it had been. The whole lower part, the old part, was gone: there was earth, and behind the earth there was water, and the water didn't even know it had done anything.

Tilde went and sat in the last room left and stayed there a while doing nothing.

And then yes, she cried, as she had said she would.

She cried about a curve she had got wrong forty years ago, about a stone she hadn't managed to move, about an empty larder: which is to say, as it always goes, about tiny things and not about the big one.

Pino said nothing, which was the hardest work of his entire life, and stayed there until it was over.

Then he said: «Are you hungry?»

And Tilde said she was.

You can lose a home without anybody having died, my love.

You can lose a school, a place you always went, a room you used to sleep in. And sometimes people say oh come on, it's only a room, and they're right, and it has nothing to do with anything.

It wasn't only a room. It was the room where you were.

You're allowed to be sad about something that didn't hurt anybody else. You don't even have to explain it.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, almost all the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

The song that stopped

A bedtime story — Book Eight, following “The room that isn't there any more”

That summer, one evening, the ceiling didn't tremble.

Tilde noticed a little late, because she had dozed off: these days it happened to her, dozing off in the evening before it was even properly dark, and waking up with no idea how much time had passed.

She waited a while, because some evenings Rocco started late.

Then she waited some more.

Then she went up under the big root and knocked three times: I'm here.

Nobody answered.

Tilde stood there with her paw raised, in a silence that had never been so silent, because it wasn't just any silence: it was the silence of a thing that had always been there.

She knocked again. Nothing.

So she dug straight up, which you never do — it ruins the ceiling, and a ruined ceiling comes down on your head sooner or later — and came out among the roots, off to one side, where the earth is dry.

Rocco was there.

He was alive, and he was very old, and the moment he saw Tilde he turned his head away.

«Hello,» said Tilde.

Rocco moved his antennae and said nothing.

Tilde waited. She was good at waiting: underground you do a great deal of it.

And after a while Rocco spoke, and the voice that came out was a thread.

«It won't come any more,» he said. «I've been trying all evening.»

Tilde didn't say try again, and didn't say never mind, and didn't say of course it will come.

She sat down.

«I can still feel you,» she said, after a bit.

«I'm not doing anything.»

«I can feel you breathing. It comes through the root.»

Rocco didn't answer, but he turned his head back a little.

They stayed there until late, saying almost nothing.

When Tilde went back down, she didn't go to bed.

She went into the corridor under the oak, put herself alongside the big root, and started thinking like a mole.

Cold air pulls from here. That was the north side: winter would come from there.

It's damp here. There was still water underneath, not much, but there.

Here, though, it isn't. Under the curve of the root, on the shaded side, there was a pocket of pale dry earth the water had never touched in all those years, because the root made a roof for it.

Tilde started digging.

It took her three nights, and she did all three in secret.

She widened the pocket by just as much as a cricket needs. She took out the little stones one by one. She smoothed the walls with her nose, the way you do for the good rooms. She left the opening small and turned to the south, so the winter wind would pass beside it instead of into it. And at the back, where the ground was softest, she pushed in a handful of dry grass she had been keeping.

Then she closed it all up behind her and went away without telling anybody.

Rocco moved in a week later.

He didn't ask who had done it. Perhaps he knew and didn't want to make a business of it, or perhaps he simply thought the place had always been there and he hadn't noticed before: that happens.

Everybody called it «the little corner of dry earth under the root of the oak», and nobody in the meadow ever wondered why the earth should be dry just there.

The song never came back.

But for all the evenings that were left, when it went dark, Tilde would go up into the corridor under the root and knock three times on the ceiling.

And from the ceiling, very faintly, three knocks would come back.

When you can't fix the thing, fix another one, my love.

Nearly always the right one can't be fixed: you can't give anybody their voice back, you can't bring back a thing that has gone.

But nearly always, somewhere near it, there's another one that can be done. Smaller, and crooked, and nothing to do with it.

Do that one. It counts just the same, and sometimes it counts more.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, almost all the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

Digging forwards

A bedtime story — Book Nine, following “The song that stopped”

Tilde spent that winter squeezed.

She fitted into the half a house she had left, but she fitted the way you fit into a coat two sizes too small: you can sleep in it, you can eat in it, you can't turn round in it.

And what she was really missing were the things you can't see.

She was missing the larder, which has to be low down and cool, or the winter stores don't keep.

And she was missing the second way out. A house with only one way out isn't a house: it's a trap with somebody inside it who doesn't know yet.

So in March, as soon as the earth went soft, Tilde sat down, looked at the east wall and said: «Right. We dig.»

«YES!» said Pino.

Pino was a shrew, and he was the most enthusiastic creature in the meadow. He did everything very badly and he did it immediately.

«Where do we start? Do we start over there? Do I start?»

«First I'll explain how it's done.»

«I know how it's done! You dig!»

«Of course you do», said Tilde. «I'll explain it anyway.»

And she taught him the thing that moles know and nobody else does.

One. You point your nose slightly upwards. Always. A tunnel that climbs, the water leaves alone; a tunnel that drops turns into a drinking glass sooner or later.

Two. You dig one mole a day. Meaning: your own length, nose to tail, and then you stop. Not two. Not three.

Three. You stop before you're tired. (Her joints, said Tilde, always got there first on this one.)

Four. A thing done in a hurry falls in.

Pino listened to all of it, nodding enormously.

And that night, while Tilde was asleep, he dug five metres on his own.

They were five beautiful metres. Wide, straight, quick, dug with a joy there's really no describing.

They fell in towards morning, with Pino inside them.

Tilde woke up at the sound, and it was a sound she knew well: not a crash, a sigh. Earth sitting down.

She didn't dig from above, because from above it falls in again.

She went round, came at it from the side and from underneath, where the ground was holding, and pulled out Pino, who was filthy, frightened, and entirely in one piece.

They sat there a moment without saying anything.

«Are you going to say it now?» said Pino, in a very small voice.

«Say what?»

«That you were right.»

«You worked it out by yourself», said Tilde. «You don't need me saying it as well.»

Then she looked at the fall, which had taken the old stretch down with it, and did the sum: a week's work under a heap of earth.

«Fine», she said. «We'll do it again.»

«All of it?»

«All of it.»

And they did it again, one mole a day.

Pino counted the nights. On the sixth he asked how much was left and Tilde said a fair bit. On the fifteenth he didn't ask. On the twenty-second he noticed something and stopped with one paw in the air.

«Tilde.»

«Mm.»

«You stop when you're not even tired yet.»

«Exactly.»

«Why?»

«Because if you stop when you're tired, you dug the last bit tired», said Tilde. «And the last bit is always the one that falls in.»

They finished in June.

It wasn't the old house. It was shorter, and plainer, with fewer bends and fewer rooms, and the larder ended up a little too close to the bed.

But it climbed in the right place, it had two ways out, and the wrong bend wasn't in it: that one had stayed on the other side, with the water, dug by a young mole forty years ago.

«Is it beautiful?» asked Pino, who knew nothing whatever about tunnels.

Tilde thought about it.

«It's straight», she said, which for a mole is the biggest compliment there is.

Then she started a new corridor towards the hedge and left it half done, the way all moles do, because a house that's completely finished is a house with nothing left to do in it.

Starting again doesn't mean hurrying, my love.

When something breaks you want to put it all back together at once, tonight, right now, and instead you nearly always put it back a little bit at a time, and for quite a while it looks as though nothing is changing.

But slowly doesn't mean stopped.

One mole a day, and by June you have a house.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, almost all the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

The city that came before

A bedtime story — Book Ten, following “Digging forwards”

Tilde went back to the half-dug corridor towards the hedge in September.

Not out of restlessness. The new house was straight and it did the job, but the larder was too close to the bed, and a larder that's warm in winter keeps nothing at all. She needed cold dry earth, and the cold dry earth was over that way, under the hedge, where she had never been.

So she dug west, one mole a day, properly.

And after a while the earth began to change.

First it went sandy, which is good news: sand dries. Then it went pale. Then it started smelling of nothing at all.

That unsettles a mole. Earth always tells you something — water came through here, a leaf died here, there's an ants' nest above this bit. That earth told her nothing, like a room nobody has walked into for a very long time.

Tilde slowed down.

Then she pushed her nose forward, and her nose didn't find earth.

It found air.

She came through with the rest of her and dropped about two moles' worth, which for an old mole is quite a lot, and lay on her back for a moment wondering whether she had broken anything.

She hadn't broken anything.

She got up, and she noticed two things straight away.

The first: she was in a corridor.

The second: she hadn't dug it.

It was wide. Wide enough for three abreast. The ceiling was a perfect round curve, the kind you only get if you know what you're doing. And the walls were smooth — not smooth the way a mole makes them on purpose: smooth the way the long corridor in your own house goes after forty years of walking down it.

But nobody had walked down this one in a very long time. You could tell.

Tilde put a paw on her chest and waited to stop being frightened.

Her head, these days, played the odd trick on her — a name that wouldn't come, a thought left half finished — but her legs never did. Her legs never forgot a single thing, and even in a brand new place like this one, they knew exactly where to put themselves.

Then, being a mole, she went to have a look.

The corridor went on. Then it opened into a four-way junction, well made, with the corners rounded off, because sharp corners wear away and fall in.

Off the junction were the rooms.

There was a deep larder, right down low, in the coldest spot, exactly where it belongs.

There was a chamber with a low round ceiling for holding the warmth in.

There was a slope going up with a right-angled bend in it, and Tilde stood looking at that for quite a while, because a bend like that is what stops water, and nobody had ever taught her that: she had worked it out herself at fifty, and she had been very pleased with herself about it.

And there was the place where the corridor went round a big stone instead of through it.

Tilde laughed quietly, on her own, underground.

She went on for an hour, and it didn't end. It was a city. Streets, junctions, rooms, exits plugged up for the winter and never opened again.

There was nobody there.

Not a sound, not a bone, not a smell: only the work, left behind.

Moles, a great many, a very long time ago. Who they were, how many, what they were called — there was no way of knowing, and there never would be.

Tilde sat down in the middle of the four-way junction and said, out loud, to nobody:

«You did a lovely job.»

The next day she brought Pino, who ran up and down for twenty minutes going oh, and then asked: «But who were they?»

«I don't know.»

«And what were their names?»

«I don't know, Pino. We'll never know.»

«So what do we do about it?»

Tilde looked at the junction, and the cold larder, and the right-angled bend got exactly right by somebody who died before she was born.

«We live in it», she said.

It took her a fortnight to join her tunnel to theirs, because that particular stretch she wanted done properly.

And Tilde's house was big again. Bigger than the old one, with the larder where a larder goes and two real ways out and a new straight piece, dug by her, joined onto an ancient piece dug by somebody else.

There was one thing she left alone.

At the end of a side branch there was a little room, round, badly made: the curve came out crooked, and you could see exactly where whoever was digging it had gone wrong and then tried to fix it.

It was somebody's first room.

Tilde left it crooked, just as it was.

You're not the first one through here, my love.

Before you there were a great many people you'll never meet, who knew nothing whatever about you: and they dug well, and they left the bends in the right places, and the streets wide, and a cold room for keeping things in.

And now you walk about in it without even noticing.

And one day it'll be your turn to leave something for somebody you'll never meet: dig it straight, please. They'll never say thank you, but they'll need it.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, almost all the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

Seven leaves

A bedtime story — Book Eleven, following “The city that came before”

One summer night, in the seventh year, Seven said: «Tilde.»

And there was something in the voice that had never been there before.

«I'm ready.»

Tilde didn't ask ready for what. She'd known for seven years.

«Now?»

«Now.»

Seven began moving upwards, slowly, with a movement she'd never made before: until then she had only stayed; now she was going.

Tilde followed her. But first she settled her spectacles properly on her nose — to do things right, even though up there she'd need them no more than she did down below, which is to say not at all.

They climbed through corridors Tilde knew by heart, and the further up they went, the more the air changed — less damp, then cold, then with something in it that Tilde didn't care for at all and which is called wind.

Tilde never went up top unless she was made to. And now she was being made to.

They reached the last stretch, the one that broke through into the open, and Seven stopped.

«I can't dig any more on my own», she said. «My legs aren't good for it now. I need somebody else to do the last bit.»

Tilde dug the last bit.

And the darkness below turned into a different kind of darkness: a darkness with air moving inside it, and sounds coming from far away instead of close by.

Outside.

Tilde didn't put her nose to it straight away.

«What's it like?» she asked.

«I don't know yet», said Seven. «Nearly there.»

They came out together, Seven first and Tilde right behind, and Tilde found herself, for the first time in seven years, standing in the meadow grass at night with nothing over her head but nothing at all.

She could hardly see anything. Moles aren't made for that, and she was perfectly happy about it.

But she could hear.

And what she heard was that the meadow, that night, was not as quiet as other nights. There was a hum, far away and close at the same time, as if the air itself were trembling a little along its whole length.

A firefly went past, and Tilde felt her go by from the change in the air.

«Hello», said the firefly. «You're the mole. You're never out here.»

«I never come up», said Tilde. «Tonight I'm seeing off a friend.»

«It's a special night, you know. The meadow's lit up all over. I've never seen anything like it. It's as if somebody's lit up a whole city, all at once, from one side to the other — there's a little girl come down from the hill and—»

«I'll take your word for it», said Tilde.

It wasn't rudeness. It was the truest thing she could say: she couldn't see any of it, and she couldn't have cared less about that, and yet she believed every word, because the firefly had no reason to lie to her.

The firefly went on, still full of wonder, towards her part of the lit-up meadow.

Tilde stayed where she was, beside Seven.

And that was when Seven began.

First a small crackle, like ice starting to split from a single point. Then the crackle stretched out, slow, very long, and it went on — Tilde couldn't have said how long, because right then time started doing something strange.

«Does it hurt?» Tilde asked, at one point.

«No», said Seven, in a voice different from before, smaller and bigger at once. «But it's long.»

«Long things are», said Tilde. «I say that all the time.»

The crackling went on. Tilde stayed put, seeing nothing, hearing everything: the sound of the old shell opening, and under that sound another sound, very small, of something new drying in the air for the first time.

Then, at some point, there was silence.

«Seven?»

«I'm here», said a voice. And it was Seven's voice, but it wasn't her old voice any more: it was a voice with something humming inside it, a small barely-there thrum, the first note of something that would grow much louder.

«How are you?»

«I have wings», said Seven. «Tilde. I have wings.»

She didn't say it as a question. She said it the way somebody says a true thing for the first time, with a voice that still doesn't quite believe it.

They stayed still a moment, Tilde on the ground and Seven beside her, wings drying slowly in the dark.

«I'm going up now», said Seven.

«I know.»

«You won't be able to come.»

«I know.»

«I don't know when I'll be back. Maybe never. Cicadas don't always come back to where they were born.»

Tilde didn't say you'll come back, and she didn't say that's all right, because she didn't know if either one was true.

She said: «Seven years is a very long time to know somebody, sitting still beside them. Go on.»

Seven stayed a moment longer, like somebody who can't quite find the strength to be the one who moves first.

Then Tilde felt the air change beside her — a movement unlike any she knew, not a crawl and not a walk: a rising.

And then she couldn't hear anything at all, and she understood that Seven had gone somewhere Tilde had no way of following, not even with her ears.

She stayed there a while longer, in the middle of the grass, under a sky she couldn't see, in a meadow that was, she'd been told, lit up all over that night.

Then she went back down.

She passed the empty cell — small, round, still holding the shape of seven years in the earth — and stopped a moment on the threshold.

She didn't close it up straight away.

She left it as it was for a while, the way you leave a door open when you still hope, just a little, that somebody might come back and knock.

Sometimes you see somebody as far as a certain point, my love, and then that point is theirs, not yours.

A first day of school. An airport. A door closing, and on the other side a whole piece of a life you'll never see, that you'll never know how it went.

It's a frightening thing, letting somebody go somewhere you can't follow.

But it's also the biggest gift you can give them: standing perfectly still right up to the edge, and not one step further, because that next step isn't yours to take.

Tilde has never seen the sky. She believed it anyway, from somebody who described it to her.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, almost all the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

The front door

A bedtime story — Book Twelve, following “Seven leaves”

The new house was almost finished.

Tilde had joined her own stretch to the ancient city's, sorted out the larder, reopened two old exits, and closed off a branch that wasn't any use to anyone any more. Only one thing was left, the thing that matters most in a house and that nobody ever thinks about until it isn't there.

The door was missing.

Or rather: she hadn't decided where the entrance would go — the exact spot in the grass where the main corridor would break through into the open air.

Tilde thought about it for two whole evenings, which for her was a very long time, because usually she decided things simply by digging and nothing else.

Then she said to Pino: «I'm putting it by the big oak root.»

«Why there?»

Tilde didn't answer straight away.

«Because that's where Rocco's room was», she said, finally. «And because I still know exactly where it is, even with my eyes shut.»

Pino didn't ask anything else. He too knew how to count the knocks that no longer came from the ceiling.

Tilde dug the last stretch calmly, the way you always do the last stretch. She came out into the open right under the shade of the oak, one step from where, for years, she'd knocked three times on a ceiling that used to answer.

And the house was finished.

That evening Pino turned up all cheerful, as usual, ready for the job he always did.

«I'm here!» he said. «Shall I walk you home?»

Because that was how it had always gone: Tilde would go out, take a few steps, turn round, find three identical tufts of grass in front of her, ask was it this one?, get told no, and then somebody would walk her home.

«No need», said Tilde.

Pino's mouth fell open.

«What do you mean, no need?»

«No need, Pino. I'm going on my own.»

And she said it with a calm that Pino didn't like one bit, because he wasn't used to that calm from her, and it felt as though something was being taken away from him.

Tilde took a few steps into the grass. She turned round.

And she found it.

First try. For the first time in her whole life.

There wasn't even a moment's doubt, not even a was it this one?: there was the entrance, exactly where she'd put it, exactly under the shade of the oak, and Tilde walked straight to it as if she'd done it a thousand times before, which, in a way, she had — only all those other thousand times it had been somebody else's house leading her there.

«But how did you do it?» asked Pino, in the small voice of somebody who has just lost a job.

Tilde thought about it, the way she'd done once before, a great many years earlier, when they'd asked her the same thing about the hedgehog in the crack.

«I never got lost because I know the grass», she said. «I got lost because I never chose the entrance myself. This one I chose. I put it where I know it always is, because it's the one place I never forget.»

Pino went quiet a moment, which for him was very rare indeed.

«And me?» he asked, after a while. «If you don't get lost any more, what am I for?»

Tilde looked at him.

«You're still for the same thing», she said. «Only now, when you come, it's because you want to. Not because I need somebody to find me.»

Pino wasn't entirely convinced, but decided he was fine with it anyway, and stayed there chatting for a while before going off to find something to eat, as usual.

Tilde stayed outside a little longer, near the new entrance, under the oak that never speaks and that, meanwhile, an inch a year, kept moving its roots exactly where it had always meant to move them.

She didn't knock the three knocks. There was nothing left above to knock for.

But she stayed there a moment anyway, the way you stay near a place that matters, even when nobody answers any more.

These days she sometimes forgot the name of a flower she'd known all her life, or why she'd come into a room. But that evening, and every evening after, she never once forgot where the door was.

Then she went in, and for the first time in her life she didn't need to tell anybody: she already knew she was home.

Home isn't where you were put, my love.

It's the one you built yourself, piece by piece, maybe getting a curve wrong the first time and fixing it the second. It's the one whose entrance you chose, and that's why you always find it again, even when everything around you looks like three identical tufts of grass.

And sometimes you put the front door exactly there, right by the memory of somebody who isn't there any more: not to stay sad, but because it's still the one spot you never forget.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

Epilogue · 5 minutes to read aloud

Epilogue

Tilde's stories — to be read after all the others

There. This one's finished too.

And I know exactly what you're about to ask, because it's the same question as always.

But is it true?

Let's do it like last time: I'll tell you what's true, piece by piece.

The meadow is true, and you know that already. Moles are true, and they really do dig like that: one tunnel a day, nose always pointing a little upwards, so the water can't take hold. Cicadas are true, and they really do spend years underground, perfectly still, before coming out for a single night with brand new wings.

And Tilde's spectacles are a little bit true too: moles really do have eyes, tiny ones, that see just about the difference between light and dark. Putting a pair of spectacles on one is a joke, but it's an honest joke, because it really wouldn't change anything even for a real mole. And the joints that ache in the cold are true as well: it can happen to old moles, the same way it can happen to anybody who gets old — not to everyone, but to plenty.

And do you remember, in the other story, when I told you that under the oak root, on the shady side, there really is a little corner of dry earth?

I told you it was true. I never told you who made it.

She did. Three nights, in secret, for a cricket who had lost his voice and to whom she couldn't give anything back, except somewhere dry to spend the winter.

Tilde, though, is the name I gave her. Like Nina.

And the well — that one was true right through. It was ours. They dug it when they built the house at the top of the hill, long before I was born, and nobody ever thought about where the water they moved would end up.

When we found out — much later, and by chance, and not from any mole, but from whoever told me this story — we couldn't put the water back where it had been. You can't, once it's found a new way. But we stopped digging any deeper, and we let that new way settle by itself, slowly, without anybody pushing it along any faster.

A little. And late. But something.

Tomorrow morning, if you like, we can go and look at the meadow as soon as it's light.

Look carefully in the grass: there are little heaps of dark earth, small, scattered here and there. People just call them “molehills” and walk straight over them without a second thought.

They're the plan of a city you'll never see. Every little heap is a piece of corridor, of room, of road — a whole world, drawn under your feet by somebody you'll never meet, and whom you barely disturb at all just by walking over it.

Don't crush them on purpose, will you? It won't help anybody, and down there, to somebody, it matters.

Now come here.

Put your hand flat on the floor, gently.

I know, you can't feel anything. You never can from up here: you have to be underground to feel what's happening underground, and that's how it should be. Not everything that's true and important is meant to be seen or heard by whoever's up top.

But it's there all the same. Now you know.

And when you're grown up, if you ever build something — a house, a well, anything at all — remember to ask who's underneath, before you dig.

«Will you tell it again tomorrow?»

Yes, my love.

Tomorrow and every evening.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

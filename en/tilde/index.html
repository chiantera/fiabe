I · 7 minutes to read aloud

The mole who couldn't find her front door

A bedtime story — Book One

In the meadow at the foot of the hill, under the grass, there lived a mole called Tilde.

Tilde was old, her coat was like worn velvet, and everybody in the meadow was fond of her. But everybody, absolutely everybody, knew one other thing about her:

that Tilde could never find her front door.

She would come out in the evening for a bit of air, take four steps, turn round, and find herself looking at three tufts of grass that were exactly the same.

«Was it this one?»

It was not that one.

«Then it was that one.»

Nor that.

It always ended with somebody walking her home: the ants, or a snail, or a firefly passing by who made her some light. And Tilde would thank them, and slip underground, and after that not a sound came up from down there.

The fireflies, among themselves, said she was a bit scatty. They said it fondly, but they said it.

Then came the night of the storm.

It rained for hours, hard, and when it stopped the meadow was full of puddles, and at one spot near the hedge the ground had opened: a narrow deep crack, with crumbling edges.

A young hedgehog had fallen into it.

He was crying very quietly, because nothing else would come out.

Everybody came. The fireflies gathered over the crack and made light, all of them together, as hard as they could.

And for the first time, the light was no use at all.

You could see the hedgehog perfectly well down there. That was precisely the problem: you could see him, and there was nothing to be done. The crack was too narrow to fly into and too deep to reach, and every time somebody leaned over the edge another piece of earth slid down.

Nobody knew what to do.

Then a firefly said: «Fetch the mole.»

And somebody answered: «Tilde? Tilde can't even find her own house.»

They fetched her anyway.

Tilde came trotting up, listened, and set off decisively in the wrong direction.

There was a very awkward silence.

Then Tilde stopped. She lowered her nose, rested it on the ground, and stayed like that.

And she stopped being funny.

«Ah,» she said. «We're above the root of the oak. So the crack is over that way.»

And she began to dig.

She was gone in three seconds, and from then on the story happened in a place where nobody from the meadow had ever been.

Because under the meadow it isn't dark: under the meadow the dark has nothing to do with anything.

Under the meadow there are roads. There are the corridors Tilde has dug all her life, and she knows them one by one, and she knows where they go the way you know where your own room is with your eyes shut. There's the smell of wet earth, which changes every two steps and tells her everything: clay this way, sand that way, a big stone above there, water running below.

And there are the roots. Tilde knows them by name: the oak's root is thick and knotted, the hedge's is thin and dense as hair, and if you follow that one you come to the crack.

Tilde followed that one.

She came up beside the hedgehog from underneath, where the earth wasn't crumbling. She dug a wide, comfortable slope, taking her time, because a thing done in a hurry falls in.

And then she said: «Come on, walk slowly. Put your feet where I put mine.»

Up above, in the meadow, they were all waiting round the crack.

But the hedgehog didn't come out of the crack.

He came out of a new hole, three metres further on, in the middle of the grass, covered in earth and alive.

And Tilde came out behind him.

«But how did you do it?» asked a firefly.

Tilde thought about it, as though she had never considered it before.

«From underneath it's all different,» she said. «From underneath I never get lost.»

Then she said goodnight to everybody, took four steps, turned round, and found herself looking at three tufts of grass that were exactly the same.

«Was it this one?»

It was not that one.

And so two fireflies walked her home, making light for her, and neither of them said she was scatty.

There's always somewhere you're very good indeed, my love.

It may not be the place where everybody is watching.

And if there's a place where you always get lost, that means nothing at all: it only means that isn't the place where you know your way.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

II · 7 minutes to read aloud

The roads under the meadow

A bedtime story — Book Two, following “The mole who couldn't find her front door”

Early one morning, while Tilde was doing the rounds of her corridors, she heard something that had never happened to her before.

She heard somebody running.

Not just the sound of feet: an actual run, back and forth, flat out, in the long corridor that goes under the hedge.

Tilde went to look.

It was a shrew.

He was tiny and grey, with a pointed nose, and he was running from one end to the other like a marble in a box.

«Good morning,» said Tilde.

The shrew stopped dead.

«Oh help,» he said. «Oh help oh help oh help. I fell in. There was a hole, I was going past, I fell in, I tried to climb out, you can't climb out, I tried that way, that way's no good, I tried this way and I think I've been this way already, and anyway I need to eat.»

«You need to eat?»

«Every twenty minutes,» said the shrew. «If I don't eat every twenty minutes I die. It's a thing about me.»

«Ah,» said Tilde. «Then we'd better get on.»

And off she went.

The shrew ran after her and started talking, and practically never stopped.

His name was Pino.

They were out in under a minute: you only had to turn left and then go up.

Pino set about eating, which is to say he vanished into a tuft of grass and made a noise of things snapping, and then popped out again very pleased and said:

«But how did you do that?»

«Do what?»

«Know which way to go! They were all the same!»

Tilde thought about it.

«They weren't the same,» she said.

And Pino, who was rude out of curiosity rather than unkindness, said: «Will you show me?»

So Tilde did something she had never done before: she showed somebody round her house.

They went down.

«This is the upper round,» said Tilde. «It's for checking. I go along it twice a day.»

«What for?»

«Because something is always falling into the corridors. A little worm, a seed, something that's taken a wrong turn. If you don't go round, you don't find anything.»

Then they went down further.

«Down here nothing falls in, and that's why it's quieter.»

Pino touched the wall. «It's smooth!»

«I made that one forty years ago,» said Tilde. «The more you go along it, the smoother it gets. You can tell how old a road is from its walls.»

They went on, and at a certain point Tilde stopped and said: «Be quiet a moment.»

With enormous effort, Pino was quiet.

And then he heard it.

A long low sound, coming from inside the earth, like putting your ear to a shell.

«What's that?»

«The brook,» said Tilde. «It runs on the other side of that wall. When the wall makes that noise it means you're under the willow, and if you're under the willow your house is to the right.»

Pino's mouth fell open.

Then they visited the last room, which was round, right at the bottom of everything, with soft walls of dry grass. That was where Tilde slept.

«This is lovely,» said Pino, speaking quietly for the first time.

And then he asked again: «But how do you remember it all?»

Tilde opened her mouth to say «I don't know», which was the answer she always gave.

But that time she stopped, and actually thought about it.

«It isn't that I remember it with my head,» she said at last. «I remember it with my feet.»

«Eh?»

«The earth changes at every step. Over there it's clay and it sticks. Over here it's sand and it slides. Under the oak it smells of mushrooms. Near the brook it's cold and heavy. Past the hedge there's a stretch full of little stones that hurt.» Tilde shrugged. «I don't decide where to go. I feel where I am.»

Pino thought about that for the whole three seconds he was able to keep still.

«Then it's easy!»

«For me it is,» said Tilde. «You try shutting your eyes in your bedroom and walking to the door.»

«I can do that!»

«There you are,» said Tilde. «Same thing. Only my house is a bit bigger.»

Then she saw him out, because twenty minutes had gone by.

There are things you can do perfectly well and couldn't explain to anybody, my love.

It doesn't mean you don't know them: it means you know them so well that you've put them away, the way you put a thing in a drawer that opens by itself when you need it.

Have a think: how many steps is it from your bed to the door?

You've no idea. But in the dark you never get it wrong.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

III · 5 minutes to read aloud

Seven

A bedtime story — Book Three, following “The roads under the meadow”

Under the meadow, in a cell about this big, attached to a thin root, there was a creature doing nothing.

She wasn't asleep. She wasn't ill. She wasn't sad.

She was simply doing nothing, and she had been doing it for seven years.

Her name was Seven, and she was a cicada nymph.

Tilde had always known her. She had found her when she was the size of a grain of rice, the year she redid the east corridor, and ever since she looked in now and then to see how things were going.

«How's it going?»

«Fine,» Seven would say.

And that was that.

One day Tilde took Pino down, who the moment he heard about the cell wanted to see it at once, and who, faced with Seven, stayed quiet for nearly two whole seconds.

Then he said: «What do you do?»

«I stay here,» said Seven.

«And then?»

«And then I stay here.»

Pino looked at Tilde, looked at the cell, looked at Seven again.

«For how long?»

«Seven years.»

Pino made a noise as though something had dropped inside his stomach.

«Seven years? What have you done in seven years?»

«This,» said Seven.

«But don't you get bored?»

And here Seven moved for the first time, only slightly, the way a person turns over in bed.

«No,» she said. «Because I'm not just sitting still. I'm becoming.»

«Becoming what?»

«I don't know yet. Something with wings.»

Pino, who in seven years would be born, grow up, grow old and die four times over, simply could not take it in.

«But it's wasted time!»

«Touch my back,» said Seven.

Pino put out a paw and touched.

«What's this hard stuff?»

«That wasn't there last year,» said Seven.

On the way out, Pino was unusually silent.

«It doesn't add up,» he said to Tilde. «Somebody sits still for seven years and something happens anyway?»

«Look around you,» said Tilde.

«I can't see anything, it's dark.»

«Exactly. Touch.»

Pino touched the corridor wall.

«That's a root,» said Tilde. «When I dug this stretch, that root wasn't there. Now it's as thick as my arm. I've never seen it move. Nobody has ever seen it move.»

They went on in the dark.

«The oak up there is fifty times your height,» said Tilde. «It came out of an acorn. Nobody ever watched it grow, not for a single afternoon. And yet there it is.»

Pino considered this, which cost him considerable physical effort.

«So the long things can't be seen while they're happening.»

«There you are,» said Tilde. «Good.»

That night Pino couldn't sleep, which for him meant staying awake twenty-two minutes together.

And every so often, in the years that followed, he would go back down to the cell on his own and sit outside it and ask: «How's it going?»

And Seven would say: «Fine.»

And he would stay a moment, and then dash off to eat.

There are things that take an enormously long time, my love.

Learning to read. Getting tall. Stopping being frightened of something.

And because they take so long, while they're happening it looks as though nothing is happening at all, and you want to say I can't do it or this is wasted time.

It isn't wasted time. It's just long.

Tonight as well, while you're asleep and doing absolutely nothing, you are becoming.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

IV · 5 minutes to read aloud

The neighbour upstairs

A bedtime story — Book Four, following “Seven”

There was one thing Tilde liked more than anything else, and she had never told anybody because she didn't know how you'd say it.

She liked it when the ceiling trembled.

It happened in summer, every evening, as soon as it went dark up above. It would start quietly, then settle, and carry on for hours: frin frin, frin frin, always the same, never identical.

For the meadow up there, that was a song.

For Tilde it wasn't a sound. Tilde has ears, but underground sounds don't amount to much: what really reaches you is the way the earth shakes. And on those evenings the earth shook in a tiny, regular way, like somebody tapping two fingers on your back to tell you they're there.

It came to her through her feet. It came through her nose, if she rested it on the floor.

Tilde had grown up with that trembling against her. She had dug half her corridors with that frin frin in the ceiling over her head.

She had never known who it was.

One night in July she decided to find out.

She went up to the place where the ceiling trembled most, which was under the big root of the oak, and there she stopped. Then she lifted a paw and knocked. Three little knocks.

Above, the frin frin stopped dead.

Tilde waited.

And after a moment, from the ceiling, came three knocks back.

Tilde stood there in the dark with her heart going, which at her age wasn't something that happened often.

Then she knocked again: two knocks.

Upstairs, two knocks.

They went on like that for half an hour, without either of them knowing who the other was and without either wondering about it too much, and then the frin frin started up again, and Tilde went back down as pleased as a child.

They actually met at the end of that summer, when she came up by mistake two steps from the root and he was out there.

He was an old cricket. His name was Rocco.

«Ah,» said Rocco. «You're the one who knocks.»

«You're the one who sings.»

«Sings?» Rocco seemed surprised. «You can hear me singing? From down there?»

«I can't hear you,» said Tilde. «I touch you.»

And since Rocco didn't understand, Tilde explained: that for her the song had never been a sound but something that came up through the earth into her feet; that she knew it by its rhythm and not by its voice; and that when he started in the evening, she knew it was time to stop working.

Rocco was quiet for a good while.

Then he said: «I sing so the ones above can hear me.»

«I know.»

«It never occurred to me it went down as well.»

«Things go downwards too,» said Tilde. «Nobody ever thinks about it.»

From that evening they had a habit, which is the best thing two who hardly ever see each other can have.

In the evening, when Rocco began, Tilde knocked three times on the ceiling: I'm here.

And Rocco, without stopping, left a tiny gap, a hole in the rhythm, which meant: me too.

Nobody in the meadow ever noticed. The fireflies said the cricket dropped a beat on certain evenings.

There's something you do that reaches somebody you don't know about, my love.

From a direction you'd never imagine, to somebody you never sent it to.

You'll almost never find out. But it happens every single time, and every so often one of them knocks to tell you.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet, the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

V · 6 minutes to read aloud

The water

A bedtime story — Book Five, following “The neighbour upstairs”

It started with nothing at all.

Tilde was doing the upper round one afternoon in June, and when she reached the west corridor she put a paw down and drew it back.

It was damp.

Not wet: damp. Like a cellar wall.

Tilde stood there for quite a while.

That corridor had been dry for twenty years. She had dug it herself in good pale earth that didn't hold water, and in twenty years nothing had ever happened there, not even after the big storms.

She ran her paw along the whole wall. Damp for four paces, then dry.

All right, she thought. A root has come through.

The following week it was damp for ten paces.

Tilde stopped doing the upper round the way she always did and began doing it another way: slowly, nose to the floor, stopping now and then to lick a finger and touch the wall and feel where the cold was pulling.

And she found that the damp wasn't a spot. It was a stripe. It came from the north — from the hill side — and ran down towards the brook across half the meadow, like the trail of a snail a hundred metres long.

And it was moving.

Every week it was a little further over.

Tilde didn't know what it was, but she knew perfectly well what it meant: the water under the meadow had changed course.

So she went up to say so.

Up above it was the finest summer anybody could remember.

The grass was tall, greener than it had ever been, full of flowers even in the places where there were usually hardly any. The ants said it was a year to tell your grandchildren about. The snails were fat and happy.

«The water is moving,» said Tilde.

«The water?» said the ants. «It hasn't rained for three weeks.»

«Underneath.»

The ants looked at one another.

«Tilde,» said one of them kindly, «look at this grass.»

And it was true, and that was exactly the problem: the grass was beautiful *because* the water was moving there. The meadow was drinking somebody else's luck without knowing it.

But that was the part Tilde couldn't get across — or rather: she said it, and nobody understood.

«And then what happens?» asked a snail.

«I don't know yet.»

«Then let's wait and see,» said the snail, very reasonably.

Tilde went back down.

Pino found her sitting in the west corridor, still, with her nose against the damp wall.

«What are you doing?»

«Listening.»

Pino put his ear to it as well. «I can't hear anything.»

«Exactly.»

«What am I supposed to hear?»

«Behind there, ten paces along, is the seam of water that used to run under the willow.» Tilde tapped twice with her paw. «I've heard it since I was born. It's the background noise of this house.»

Pino listened harder.

«It's gone?»

«It's further away,» said Tilde. «By quite a bit.»

They were quiet a moment.

«But if everything's fine up above,» said Pino, «maybe it isn't serious.»

«Maybe,» said Tilde.

And that night, for the first time in her life, she went to sleep in a different room: the highest one, nearest the way out.

She didn't tell anybody, and she didn't explain it to herself either. It simply seemed like a good idea.

There are times when you notice something before everybody else, my love.

And when that happens hardly anyone believes you, because they look out of the window and outside there's sunshine.

It doesn't mean you're wrong. It only means you were looking from somewhere else, and from there you see it sooner.

Tell them anyway. And in the meantime, if it feels right, sleep wherever you sleep best.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet — almost everything — the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

VI · 6 minutes to read aloud

The ones up there

A bedtime story — Book Six, following “The water”

In the end Tilde decided to go and see.

Not up: down. She wanted to follow the damp stripe back to where it started, and it started to the north, on the hill side, where she had never been because there had never been anything to do there.

She prepared for the journey the way you prepare for serious journeys, which is by eating a great deal and telling nobody.

Pino found out anyway and insisted on coming, and after two hours he said he was hungry and went back, and Tilde carried on alone.

It took her six days.

Not because it was far, but because digging uphill is another business entirely: the earth comes down at you instead of going away, and you have to stop often, and a sloping tunnel dug in a hurry falls in.

So she went: a stretch, a rest, a stretch, a rest. And at every stop she put her nose to the wall and checked she was still on the stripe.

The earth changed as she went.

First it got stonier. Then colder. Then, on the fourth day, it changed taste altogether: it tasted of iron and of things stirred about, like a badly made cake.

Tilde stopped, because that was earth somebody had taken out and put back.

It hadn't been a mole. A mole packs the earth behind her and leaves it firm. This had been tipped in, in big lumps, with stones in it that had no business being there and a piece of something hard and smooth that wasn't a stone.

On the fifth day she found the wall.

Not a wall of hard earth: a real wall, straight and cold, going down and down and never ending. Tilde walked along beside it for a long way, touching it, and understood she was going round something enormous.

It was the new house, from below. The foundations.

And on the sixth day, following the cold, she found the thing.

It was a pipe. Round, smooth, as wide as she was, and it went straight down, far further down than Tilde had ever been in her life: with her nose against it she could feel air rising from below, and with the air a sound of water, very distant and very deep.

Tilde stayed there a long time, working it out.

Then she worked it out, and working it out was no satisfaction at all.

The water under the meadow hadn't gone off on its own. The ones up there had made a hole to go and fetch it, and water, which always goes wherever the way is easiest, had stopped running under the willow and now came here.

And then, of course, it had to come out somewhere.

And it was coming out in the middle of the meadow, slowly, spreading a little wider every week.

Tilde started back.

And on the way back the worst part of the whole business came to her, the part that hurt more than the water did: there was nobody to be angry with.

If it had been a storm, it was the storm. If it had been a badger caving in her corridors, it was the badger.

But up there they didn't even know the meadow had an underneath.

They had made a hole to drink from, which is the most ordinary thing in the world, and they hadn't the faintest idea they had moved a river through somebody's ceiling.

They weren't wicked. They were only big, and distracted, and above.

That same summer, up there at the top of the hill, a window stayed lit all night, and an old firefly set off from the meadow to go and see what it was.

Tilde never knew, and knowing would have done her no good at all.

She got home on the ninth day, more tired than she had ever been, crawled into the highest room and slept for two whole days.

Then she got up, and began to think about where to move house.

Sometimes it's nobody's fault, my love.

That's the hardest thing of all to swallow, much harder than when somebody is to blame: because if there's somebody to blame you can at least be angry, and being angry is comfortable.

Instead, sometimes a thing has simply happened, and all that's left is to say: all right. And then to see what can be done.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, the roads are almost all where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

I · 6 minutes to read aloud

The bat nobody could hear

A bedtime story — Book One

Above the meadow at the foot of the hill, at night, there flew somebody nobody had ever heard.

His name was Ugo, and he was a bat.

Ugo had eyes, and he could see with them too — bats aren't blind, whatever everybody says. But to get about in the dark he used something else.

He used his voice.

It worked like this: Ugo shouted. A small, very high shout, so high that nobody could hear it: not the fireflies, not the crickets, not people. The shout went out into the dark, bumped into things, and came back.

And from the way it came back, Ugo knew what the world was like.

The oak came back round, full of holes and trembling leaves. The hedge came back thick, like shouting into a sponge. The brook came back smooth, with a shimmer on top. A mosquito came back as a little dot that moved — and then Ugo turned this way, opened his mouth, and the mosquito didn't come back any more.

So Ugo knew the meadow better than anybody. He heard all of it, every night, piece by piece.

And nobody knew he existed.

He hadn't always been on his own.

Once he had lived in a hollow tree, on the far side of the brook, with lots of others like him: the colony. In the daytime they slept hanging upside down, squeezed tight together to keep warm. And in the evening they all went out at once, and the air filled with very high voices calling to each other and answering, and for anybody who could hear them it was the most beautiful sound in the world.

Then, one autumn, Ugo went hunting a long way off. Further than usual, where there were still plenty of mosquitoes. And when he came back, the tree was empty.

Every year, with the first cold, the colony left for a faraway cave to spend the winter. That year they had left one night early. And Ugo wasn't there.

He called them all night long.

And only his own voice came back.

He spent the winter alone, in the empty tree, sleeping the way bats sleep in winter: a great deal, with his heart going very slowly. In spring he waited for them to come back. They didn't. Nor the spring after. Sometimes colonies change house, and they don't tell anybody.

Ugo stayed.

Every evening he went out and called. At first he called for them. Then, after a while, he just called, because a bat always calls: that's how he knows where he is.

Then, one spring night, something new happened.

He was flying over the hill, and his voice came back with a shape he had never heard before.

It came back straight.

Nothing in the meadow is straight. The grass bends, the stones are round, the leaves are crooked, and even the brook takes a bend whenever it can. But up there, at the top of the hill, his voice came back all at once, smooth and exact, like a ruled line.

Ugo went closer.

It was a house. A new house, just finished, that still smelt of wood and plaster. Nobody lived in it: the windows were dark, and inside you couldn't hear anything breathing.

Under the edge of the roof, where the tiles stopped, there was a hole. Exactly his size.

Ugo went in.

Inside it was dark, and dry, and still warm from the day's sun. And when Ugo shouted, to hear what the place was like, his voice came straight back, close and kind, like somebody answering you from the next room.

Ugo hung himself from a beam, upside down. He wrapped himself in his wings like a blanket.

«Goodnight,» he said to the roof.

And the roof answered at once, in his own voice: goodnight.

It wasn't the same as the colony. But it was close.

Outside it was starting to get light, which for a bat is bedtime. And Ugo fell asleep, for the first time in a long while, somewhere that felt like home.

Even when nobody answers you, my love, something answers all the same.

The wall, the tree, the hill. If you call, they send your voice back to you, and from the way it comes back you know where you are.

It isn't the same as a real answer. But in the meantime it tells you where you are. And from there, you can wait.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

II · 5 minutes to read aloud

The ones down there

A bedtime story — Book Two, following “The bat nobody could hear”

For a whole summer, the house at the top of the hill belonged to Ugo alone.

He knew it beam by beam. He knew which tile creaked in the wind, where the afternoon warmth collected, and in which corner his voice came back roundest. An empty house answers loudly: every room sends the whole shout back to you, as if it couldn't wait to talk to somebody.

Then, one morning at the end of summer, while Ugo was asleep upside down, the house filled with noise.

Doors. Boxes. Footsteps up and down the stairs. Something enormously heavy dragged across the floor, and somebody saying careful, careful, careful.

Ugo opened one eye, listened, and shut it again. In the daytime a bat doesn't bother with anything.

But in the evening, when he went out, the house answered him differently.

It answered softly. The curtains, the beds, the coats hanging behind the door each took a little piece of his voice, and what came back was gentler, and shorter. That's how a full house answers: like somebody with a lot to do who listens to you anyway.

The ones down there had arrived.

Ugo hardly ever saw them. In the daytime he slept; at night they were inside and he was out. But he heard them through the ceiling, and since he didn't know their names, he called them by their sounds.

There was Heavy Step, who went up the stairs two at a time and made the beams shake.

There was Teaspoon: every evening, at the same time, from the kitchen there came a tink tink tink of a teaspoon stirring sugar in a cup. Always three times round. Never four.

And there was the smallest sound of all.

It came from the room right under Ugo's beam. It was breathing. Short and quick: somebody small, awake while everyone else was asleep.

Ugo called them Breath.

Breath had a lamp, and kept it on all night. Ugo knew because every evening, as he came out of the hole under the tiles, he saw the window just below: a yellow square, the only lit one on the whole hill.

And the first evening he flew past it, Ugo discovered something wonderful.

The lamp called the mosquitoes.

They came from all over the meadow, drawn by the light and by the warm breath coming through the half-open window. They whined against the glass, looked for a gap, queued up to get in.

Ugo had never seen — or rather, never heard — so many mosquitoes in one place.

From that evening on, Breath's window became his favourite place to hunt. He went back and forth in front of the glass, slowly, precisely, and every mosquito that arrived with the idea of getting in changed its mind for good.

Not one mosquito bit Breath, all summer long. Not a single one.

One evening, through the ceiling, Ugo heard Heavy Step say to Teaspoon: «Funny, though. Not one mosquito up here.»

«Must be the hill air,» said Teaspoon. Tink tink tink.

Ugo, out there with his mouth full, said nothing. Nobody would have heard him anyway.

Then, at dawn, he went back in through the hole, hung from his beam, and listened.

Underneath him, Breath was still awake. Still short, still quick. The lamp still on.

Ugo knew how to send mosquitoes away. But not that short breathing. He didn't know how yet.

Somebody takes care of you without you knowing, my love.

While you sleep there's somebody closing a window, somebody tucking in a blanket, somebody sending away a thing that whines. You don't see it, and the next day nobody tells you.

You don't need to say thank you. You only need to sleep. That's just fine.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

III · 6 minutes to read aloud

The noise in the ceiling

A bedtime story — Book Three, following “The ones down there”

There was a problem, and Ugo noticed it soon enough.

Every evening, to go out, he had to get from his beam to the hole under the tiles. And bats walk terribly. They're made for flying, not for tiptoeing: they drag themselves along on folded wings, grip with their claws, slip, grip again.

On the boards of the roof, this made a noise.

Scritch. Scritch-scritch. Tick.

Right above Breath's room. And right at the time Breath was being put to bed.

The first evening Ugo didn't notice. The second evening he heard the breathing down there get shorter. The third evening he heard it go muffled, as if it were coming from underneath something.

Ugo knew what that meant. From the way sounds come back, he understood everything: Breath had hidden under the blanket.

Because of him.

So he tried to be quiet. He tried sliding instead of gripping, moving one claw at a time, even holding his breath. No good: scritch, scritch-scritch, tick.

He tried going out later, when Breath was asleep. But Breath was never asleep, and meanwhile the mosquitoes at the window were waiting, and a hungry bat is a bat who makes even more noise.

One evening, through the ceiling, he heard Breath's voice, very small:

«There's something in the ceiling.»

«There's nothing there,» said Teaspoon, who had come to say goodnight. «Go to sleep.»

«But I can hear it.»

«It'll be the wind,» said Heavy Step, from further off.

It wasn't the wind. It was Ugo. And Ugo, hanging from his beam, wouldn't have known how to say so.

Breath's room was right under the roof, with a low, slanting ceiling. Standing up on the bed, you could reach it with your hand.

And one night, after the usual scritch-scritch, Breath did something they had never done before.

Breath stood up on the bed. Trembling. Ugo could hear it in the breathing, going back and forth like a leaf.

And knocked on the ceiling. Three times.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Ugo kept perfectly still.

Because he knew those three knocks.

He had been hearing them for years, every summer evening, at the far end of the meadow: three little knocks coming from under the ground, near the root of the oak, and straight after them a tiny gap in a cricket's song. He didn't know who was knocking or who was answering. But he had understood a long time ago what they meant.

Three knocks mean: I'm here.

Ugo stretched out the claw on his wing, slowly, and knocked on the board beneath him.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Below, silence.

Breath's breathing stopped altogether, the way you hold the air in before you jump into water.

Then, after a very long moment, two knocks.

Knock. Knock.

Ugo knocked twice.

Below, once.

Ugo, once.

And then, down there, the thing happened that Ugo had been waiting for all summer without knowing it. The breathing got longer. Once, twice, three times. Slow, heavy, peaceful.

Breath was asleep.

The lamp stayed on. That was no business of Ugo's, and he knew it. But from that evening on, the ceiling didn't frighten anybody.

Quite the opposite.

Every evening, as Ugo got ready to go out, three knocks came up from below. And he answered, three knocks. And only then did he go out, scritch, scritch-scritch, tick, and now that noise just meant: off to supper, back soon.

One evening he heard Breath say to Teaspoon:

«There's somebody in the ceiling.»

«No, sweetheart. There's nothing there.»

«Yes there is,» said Breath. «But they're kind.»

Something frightening, my love, is sometimes only frightening because you don't know who it is.

If you speak to it and it answers, it's a little less frightening. And if it answers every evening, sooner or later it becomes company.

You can't always knock. But when you can, try it: sometimes on the other side there's somebody who was only waiting for you to ask.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

IV · 6 minutes to read aloud

The long sleep

A bedtime story — Book Four, following “The noise in the ceiling”

In November the mosquitoes ran out.

Not all at once: one evening there were ten at Breath's window, then five, then a single one whining softly, as if it were a bit cold too. Then none.

And Ugo knew what that meant.

The long sleep was coming.

Every bat has it. When it gets cold and there's nothing left to eat, the body starts slowing down by itself: the heart beats slowly, then more slowly, then so slowly you can hardly hear it; the wings fold up like an umbrella; and you sleep. Not for a night, not for a week. You sleep until March.

There's no arguing with the long sleep. It comes the way snow comes: it doesn't ask whether you agree.

But that year Ugo had a problem he had never had before.

The three knocks.

Every evening, from below, Breath knocked. And every evening Ugo answered. It had become their thing, like Teaspoon's tink tink tink, like Heavy Step's two stairs at a time.

If Ugo slept until March, who would answer?

Breath would knock, and the ceiling would stay silent. One evening, two, ten. And in the end Breath would think the one in the ceiling had gone away.

Ugo would have liked to explain. But how do you explain a thing like that, with three knocks?

On the last evening he tried anyway. When he heard the knocking, he answered. Then he knocked three more times, very slowly, with long silences in between: the way somebody knocks when they're terribly sleepy. It was the closest thing to goodnight, see you in spring that a bat could say with one claw.

Below, silence. Who knows whether Breath had understood.

Then Ugo tried to stay awake.

He counted the beams. He counted the tiles. He hung from one foot only, just to be uncomfortable. He went over all the shapes of the meadow in his head: the round oak, the thick hedge, the smooth brook.

He got as far as the brook. Then he didn't get anywhere any more.

He was asleep.

That winter it snowed. The snow settled on the roof, white and silent, and under the tiles the cold grew so deep it was like a room of its own.

Ugo, hanging from his beam, wrapped in his wings, heard nothing. His heart beat every now and then, like somebody remembering something and then forgetting it again.

And underneath him, every evening, somebody knocked three times.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

And nobody answered.

Some evenings the knocks were loud, almost cross. Some evenings they were small, like a question asked under your breath. Some evenings there was only one, and then nothing more. But the next evening there were three again.

All winter long.

In March, one evening, Ugo opened an eye.

His heart started again slowly, like a clock somebody winds one turn at a time. His wings were stiff, his legs were numb, and he was hungry enough to eat every mosquito on the hill — though there weren't any yet.

He dragged himself towards the hole. Scritch. Scritch-scritch.

And before he had even got to the tick, from below:

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Straight away. As if somebody had been waiting there with their hand already raised.

Ugo stopped. He stretched out his claw, still a little crooked from sleep, and answered.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

And from below came a sound Ugo had never heard in all that time.

Breath was laughing.

«Who are you talking to?» asked Teaspoon, from the door.

«The one in the ceiling,» said Breath. «They'd gone to sleep.»

«All winter?»

«All winter. I knew it.»

Somebody who's asleep hasn't gone away, my love.

Sometimes somebody you love goes away for a while: to sleep, to work, far off. And for a while you knock, and nobody answers.

It doesn't mean they're not there any more. It only means it's winter.

And winters end. All of them.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

V · 7 minutes to read aloud

The night Pino was quiet

A bedtime story — Book Five, following “The long sleep”

In the meadow there was somebody who never stopped talking.

Ugo had known him for years, though he had hardly ever seen him. He heard him. Every night, flying low over the grass, his voice came back from one spot in the meadow with the same sound in it every time: words, words, words, the crunch of something being eaten, and then more words.

He was a shrew, and his name was Pino.

Pino talked to everybody. He talked to the ants, who didn't answer. He talked to the stones. He talked to an old mole who lived under the meadow and who, it has to be said, sometimes answered. And when there was nobody about, he talked to himself, because as far as he was concerned a thought left unsaid was a thought wasted.

And Ugo had noticed something.

Sometimes, when he talked too fast, Pino made squeaks so high, so thin, that to Ugo they sounded almost like words in his own language. Nobody else in the meadow went up that high.

So perhaps, Ugo thought, Pino could hear him.

One evening he tried. He dropped low over him and said: «Hello.»

Pino didn't even notice. He was explaining to a stone why dandelion seeds are the tastiest.

Ugo tried again on other evenings. Nothing. It wasn't that Pino couldn't hear him: it was that to hear somebody you have to be quiet, if only for a moment, and Pino never had that moment.

Then came the night of the owl.

It was a moonless night, and nobody in the meadow noticed a thing, because an owl flies without making a sound. Her feathers are soft right to the tips, and the air goes through them as if they weren't there. The fireflies didn't hear her, the crickets didn't hear her.

Only Ugo heard her, with his echo: a big, soft shape gliding over the grass, turning, coming back. She was looking for something.

And in the middle of the meadow, in the most open spot of all, was Pino. Talking.

«...and so I said to her, look, the biggest seed isn't necessarily the tastiest, quite the opposite, and she goes...»

The owl turned. She dropped lower.

«Down!» shouted Ugo.

Pino didn't hear. He was talking.

«...and anyway tomorrow I'm going to look under the hedge, because if you ask me...»

«Down! Pino, down!»

Nothing. The owl dropped lower still, wide and silent, and Ugo heard her circle tighten, the way a thread tightens round a finger.

And just then something happened that had never happened in the meadow before.

Pino found a seed. He stopped. And he started to chew.

And for the first time all night, around Pino, there was silence.

«DOWN!»

Pino heard it. A tiny, very high voice, thin as a needle, coming from the sky. He didn't understand where from. He didn't understand whose. But he flattened himself into the grass so fast that the seed was still in his mouth.

The owl passed over him, a hand's breadth above the tips of the grass. She found nothing. She made one more circle, then flew off towards the hill.

The owl wasn't wicked. She was hungry, the way a storm has rain in it. But that evening, luckily, she had her supper somewhere else.

Pino stayed flat for quite a while. Then, very slowly, he said:

«Who said that?»

And he stayed quiet, waiting for the answer. Two whole seconds. A thing never seen before.

«Me,» said Ugo.

«Me who?»

«Up here.»

Pino looked up and saw nothing, because it was dark and because shrews can't see much. But he had understood the important thing. He had understood the rule.

«Right, let's do it like this,» he said. «I talk, then I'm quiet, and you answer. All right?» And he was quiet.

«All right,» said Ugo.

It was the first conversation of his life with somebody who wasn't a wall.

For weeks Pino told the whole meadow about a voice from the sky that had saved his life. Nobody believed him. The ants said he'd dreamt it. The stones said nothing, as usual. Only an old mole listened to him all the way to the end, and didn't laugh.

From that night on, every so often, towards evening, Pino would lie on his back in the middle of the grass and say: «Ugo?»

And then he would be quiet. On purpose.

For him it was the hardest thing in the world. But he did it.

To hear somebody you have to be quiet, now and then, my love.

There are small voices that only get through when everything else is silent: the voice of somebody who loves you, the voice of somebody who wants to warn you about something, and sometimes even your own.

Try it tonight. Be silent for a moment, and listen to what comes.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

VI · 7 minutes to read aloud

The world upside down

A bedtime story — Book Six, following “The night Pino was quiet”

Pino had a question.

Pino always had a question, but this one had been going round in his head for days. And one evening, lying on his back in the middle of the grass, he asked it.

«Ugo?»

Silence. On purpose.

«Yes?»

«Is it true you sleep upside down?»

«Yes.»

«All day?»

«All day.»

«And nothing falls out? Doesn't all your blood go to your feet? I mean, to your head? Doesn't it feel like the sky's on the ground? And your dreams, are they upside down too? And if you need to sneeze, where does the sneeze go?»

Then he remembered the rule, and was quiet.

«I don't know,» said Ugo. «I've never thought about it. I'm comfortable like that.»

Pino wasn't convinced. To Pino, comfortable meant one thing only: a dry hole, a little heap of leaves, and your tummy on the ground. How can anybody be comfortable hanging by their feet?

So he decided to try.

The first night he climbed up a blade of grass. The blade of grass bent over, and Pino found himself on the ground, on his back, with grass in his mouth.

«Too soft,» he said.

The second night he tried a twig in the hedge. He gripped it with his back legs, let himself go little by little... and there he stayed, upside down, swinging like a pear.

«There!» he shouted. «There! I've done it! Ugo, look, I've done it!»

Then his feet slipped, and he fell into the leaves.

«I don't understand,» he said, from the leaves. «I don't understand how you do it. And most of all I don't understand why.»

And he was quiet. Because this time he really wanted the answer.

So Ugo explained. It took a while, because every so often Pino forgot the rule and they had to wait for him to finish.

He explained that a bat can't run. His legs are small and crooked, made for holding on, not for walking. A bird, to take off, jumps. A firefly lets go of a blade of grass. But a bat on the ground, to fly, would need a run-up: and since he can't run, it's a mess.

Hanging upside down, though, he doesn't have to do a thing. He opens his wings, lets go, and he's already flying. Like somebody who wakes up and finds they're already dressed.

«And besides,» said Ugo, «up there under the roof nobody comes looking for me. Anyone who wanted to eat me would have to walk on the ceiling.»

Pino thought about it. He imagined himself walking on the ceiling, and it made him laugh.

«So you sleep upside down because that way you're already ready to go.»

«Yes.»

«And you're safe.»

«Yes.»

«And you're comfortable.»

«Yes. For me it's the most comfortable way there is.»

«But not for me.»

«Not for you.»

Pino was quiet for a very long moment, for him.

«Then both ways are fine,» he said.

Then, because he was Pino, he wanted to try one last time anyway.

He climbed up a low root, very very low, so that if he fell he wouldn't fall far. He hung by his back legs. He let himself go.

And the world turned over.

The grass hung down from above like a fringe. The sky was down below him: a great black pool full of stars, so deep it looked as if you could fall right into it. And the house at the top of the hill was hanging from the meadow by its roof, just like Ugo from his beam.

«Ugo,» said Pino, in a small voice. «It's beautiful.»

«I know.»

«My head's spinning.»

«I know that too. At first.»

«Did yours spin too?»

Ugo thought about it. He couldn't remember. Perhaps it had, a long time ago, the very first time, in the middle of the others.

«Perhaps,» he said.

Pino stayed hanging a little longer, looking at the sky underneath. Then he climbed down, settled into his leaves tummy-down, with his tummy on the ground, just how he liked it, and gave a happy sigh.

«The world upside down is beautiful,» he said. «But I sleep the right way up.»

«And I sleep the wrong way up,» said Ugo.

And they both went to sleep: Pino that very night, and Ugo the next morning, when the light came. Because that's upside down too, for a bat: light means it's time for bed.

Everybody has their own way of being comfortable, my love.

Some people sleep on their tummy and some on their back, some with the light on and some in the dark, some curled up like a hedgehog and some spread out like a star.

Your way might look strange to somebody. That doesn't mean it's wrong: it only means that somebody, to be comfortable, does it differently.

Tonight, find yours, and settle into it.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

VII · 7 minutes to read aloud

The lamp goes out

A bedtime story — Book Seven, following “The world upside down”

Ugo had always known the fireflies.

He met them every summer night, above the meadow: hundreds of little lights coming on and going off slowly, as if they were breathing. He could hear them with his echo too, small and soft, and he could see them with his eyes, because bats can see perfectly well, whatever people say.

But he never ate them. Never.

Fireflies taste dreadful: Ugo had learnt that when he was young, just once, and once was enough. Ever since, to him, that light was a sign. It meant: not for you. And he flew past them, wide and polite, the way you walk past a garden with a sign saying keep off the grass.

Apart from that, the fireflies stayed down in the meadow and he stayed up above. They had never said anything to each other. They wouldn't have heard him anyway.

One summer evening Ugo was in his spot: in front of Breath's window, hunting. The lamp was on, as always. The mosquitoes were queuing against the glass, as always.

And from the far end of the meadow a little light came up.

Just one. It flew slowly, a bit crooked, like somebody who has come a long way and never made that journey before. It crossed the brook, crossed the hedge, climbed up over the tall grass. And it landed on the glass.

Ugo swerved wide, as always. Not for you.

But as he passed, his echo told him everything. He heard the firefly clinging to the glass, tiny, its wings trembling with the effort. He heard, beyond the glass, the lamp, warm. And he heard Breath, in bed, with the covers pulled up to their nose. Short and quick, as always.

Then the little light on the glass came on. Not bright: soft and warm, like a breath of its own.

And Breath, in there, changed.

It stopped. Then it started again, but slower. Ugo knew Breath well enough to understand what that meant: Breath was looking at something, and wasn't afraid.

It stayed like that for a very long time.

Then, from inside the room, a small sound.

Click.

The lamp went out.

For the first time since the house had existed, Breath's window was dark.

And at the bottom of the hill, all at once, the meadow lit up.

Ugo had never seen anything like it. Two hundred little lights appeared in the grass in a single moment, like a town seen from above. The brook began to shine. The oak stood out black and round against everything else. They had been there every evening, and he had flown over them a thousand times: but next to that lit window, from up high, you could hardly see them.

That night, coming back under the tiles at dawn, Ugo heard Breath sleeping.

And the breathing was longer than it had ever been.

It was only the next day, at suppertime, that Ugo understood what all this cost.

The window was dark that evening too. And with no lamp, the mosquitoes didn't come to the window any more.

His favourite spot was finished.

For a few evenings he went back anyway, out of habit: to and fro in front of the dark glass, waiting for a queue that never came. Then he gave up, and went hunting over the brook.

It was further away. It was colder. The mosquitoes were scattered about, and he had to chase them one by one instead of finding them all in a row, waiting. Ugo came home in the mornings more tired, and sometimes still a bit hungry.

And yet he was happy.

He couldn't quite explain why. He only knew that every evening, as he came out of the hole, the window below went dark and the meadow down there lit up, as if they were saying goodnight to each other. And he knew that underneath him, now, somebody was really sleeping.

He had lost his favourite spot. And in exchange he lived above a ceiling that people slept well under.

It seemed like a good bargain to him.

That same summer something else happened too, and Ugo never understood it.

Some nights, flying low along the walls of the house, he heard a deep scraping, under the ground. Something digging slowly, round the foundations, a little at a time, stopping often, like somebody tired or somebody with old legs.

Ugo dropped right down to the grass and called.

And only his own voice came back.

The scraping went on for a few nights. Then it moved off towards the meadow, and wasn't heard again.

Ugo never found out who it was. Sometimes, still, he wondered.

Sometimes somebody else's good news costs you something, my love.

Somebody finds their courage, and you lose a spot. Somebody is happy, and you have to go the long way round.

It doesn't mean the news is bad. It only means that sometimes lovely things have a small price, and that sometimes you're the one who pays it.

And that you can be happy anyway.

And now close your eyes.

VIII · 9 minutes to read aloud

The meadow holds its breath

A bedtime story — Book Eight, following “The lamp goes out”

Ever since the lamp had gone out, Ugo's evenings always began the same way.

He came out of the hole under the tiles, scritch, scritch-scritch, tick, and at that very moment, down below, Breath's window went dark. And away at the bottom of the hill the meadow lit up all at once.

Ugo had grown fond of it. It almost felt as if he were the one lighting it, just by coming out.

Then, one evening, the window went dark, the meadow lit up, and something new happened.

The door opened.

Ugo knew that door. He heard it in the daytime, in his sleep, when the ones down there came and went. But at night it had never opened.

And out of the door came footsteps. Not Heavy Step, not Teaspoon. Short, uncertain steps that stopped every so often and then set off again.

And with the steps, a breath. Short and quick, like on the very first evenings.

Breath was going down the hill. Alone. In the dark.

Ugo followed from above, wide and silent, the way you follow somebody who mustn't know they're being followed.

Breath went down slowly. The tall grass came up to their tummy. At one point a white light came on, hard and straight, sweeping to and fro: a torch. It didn't bother Ugo; he looked with his voice.

But in the meadow, somebody else took fright.

Because to anyone who lives in the grass, whoever comes down the hill isn't small. They're a giant. The grass bends at every step, the ground shakes. And the fireflies, who a moment before had been a town full of lights, did something Ugo had never seen them do.

They went out. All together. Two hundred lanterns gone in a second.

Breath reached the middle of the meadow and stopped.

The torch searched everywhere: in the grass, in the hedge, on the stones in the brook. It found nothing. Then it went out too, and Ugo heard a rustle: Breath had sat down in the wet grass.

And the breathing became the shortest it had ever been.

Ugo knew it too well to be wrong. Breath was afraid. They had come all the way down here, in the dark, alone, to see the lights. And the lights had vanished. And now all around there was only black, and the black seemed empty.

But Ugo, from above, called.

And his voice came back full.

The dark wasn't empty at all. Under every clover leaf there was a firefly with its lantern closed, small and still, holding its breath. Two hundred fireflies holding their breath. Under the root of the oak there was an old cricket with his antennae raised, listening. There was a snail halfway across a stone. There was the brook, carrying on as always. And somewhere in the grass, for once in his life, even Pino was quiet.

The whole meadow was there, all around Breath. The whole meadow was keeping still, and waiting.

Ugo would have liked to tell them. You're not alone. They're all here. They're very close.

But there was no ceiling to knock on. And his voice, he knew, wouldn't be heard.

So he did the only thing he could. He stayed. He circled over the meadow, wide and slow, for as long as it took. The way you stay close to somebody even when they don't know you're there.

A very long time passed. Breath didn't move. And little by little, Ugo heard the breathing change: less short. Less quick. Like somebody who, instead of being afraid of the dark, begins to listen to it.

And then, in the dark, a single little light came on.

Then another, pale green, two steps further on. Then three. Then ten. Then the whole meadow, a piece at a time, the way a town lights up when evening comes.

And Breath's breathing stopped altogether. And then it started again, long, very long, as if Breath had been waiting their whole life to breathe like that.

From deep in the grass a song rose up, chirr, crooked and thin and beautiful. And Ugo, up above, understood that he wasn't needed any more that night. And he went off to have his supper.

But the night wasn't over.

Later, hunting on the far side of the meadow, where the grass is shorter, Ugo heard a voice. An old voice, slow and calm, talking to a firefly. The firefly was terribly excited, and was saying the meadow was lit up as never before, and that somebody had come down from the hill...

«I'll take your word for it,» said the old voice.

Ugo didn't know whose voice it was. He had never heard it outside the ground.

And soon afterwards, from the same spot, came a long, long sound. A crackling, like ice when it starts to split from a single point and then doesn't stop. Something, down there under the grass, was opening.

Ugo didn't go closer. Some things are done alone, or in twos.

At dawn, heading back towards the roof, tired and with a full tummy, Ugo passed something in the sky he had never heard before.

It was flying upwards. Badly, crookedly, the way somebody flies when they're doing it for the very first time. Its wings, to his echo, were still soft. And inside it, just barely, something hummed.

Ugo moved aside and let it go by.

Then he slipped into the hole under the tiles, tick, and hung himself from his beam.

Below him, in bed, Breath was back. And sleeping long.

That night, all over the meadow, everyone had had their own story. The one who came down the hill. The one who lit up first. The one who listened from under a root. The one who took somebody's word for it. The one who grew wings.

And only one, up above, had heard them all together.

Nobody knew. Nobody had asked him. But before he fell asleep Ugo went over them all, one by one, the way you count precious things.

When you keep still in the dark, my love, you're never as alone as you think.

The dark seems empty because you can't see anything. But it's full: of somebody breathing, of somebody waiting, of somebody holding their breath along with you.

Tonight, if you wake up and it's dark, remember that. There are more people around you than you can see.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

IX · 8 minutes to read aloud

Somebody turned

A bedtime story — Book Nine, following “The meadow holds its breath”

There's something Ugo never told anybody, not even Pino.

In the meadow, hardly anyone could hear his voice. But hardly anyone doesn't mean no one.

Some moths could hear it. And some crickets.

Ugo knew because, when he called, they all did the same thing: they fled. The moths dropped into the grass like stones. The crickets leapt away, far off, and hid under a leaf. To them a bat's voice didn't mean hello. It meant look out.

They were the only ones in the whole meadow made on purpose to hear him. And they heard him in order to run away.

Pino was different: Pino heard him by chance, and had nothing to fear. But the ones whose ears were made for him had never stopped to listen. Not even once.

Ugo didn't take it personally. That was how it should be: a moth has to be a moth. But some evenings, when one dropped into the grass the moment he opened his mouth, it made him a little sad all the same.

There was one cricket, though, that Ugo loved without the cricket knowing.

He lived under the root of the oak. He was the one with the three knocks: when somebody knocked from under the ground, his song would stop for a moment, as if to answer. And one night many years before, flying over the brook, Ugo had heard him say something to somebody, sitting on a stone:

«In the dark, sometimes a voice is all you need to know where to go.»

Ugo had never forgotten it. It was the truest thing he had ever heard anyone say who wasn't a bat.

Then, one summer, that cricket had stopped singing. Since then, under the root, there had been only silence.

One September evening Ugo was hunting over the meadow, and heard something in the sky he had never heard before.

It wasn't a bird. It wasn't a moth. It was a little cloud of small, soft things, all lit up, rising slowly. Forty fireflies, holding tight to one another.

And in the middle, carried the way you carry something that might break, was an old cricket.

The cricket from the root.

Ugo swerved very wide, so as not to frighten the fireflies. But to fly he had to call, and he called softly, as softly as he could.

And the cricket heard him.

Ugo noticed at once. He could tell from the way the cricket went stiff, up there among the fireflies. It was what every cricket did when it heard his voice, a moment before running away.

Only this cricket couldn't run away. He was in the sky, old, with legs that wouldn't hold him any more.

And Ugo thought: now he'll be frightened. Now the fireflies will feel him trembling, and they'll be frightened too. And he moved further off.

But the cricket didn't tremble.

He turned his head, slowly. And he turned one antenna, just one, towards the dark where Ugo's voice was coming from.

And he raised it. Up.

Ugo didn't know that for that cricket, up meant yes. Nobody knew, except one firefly.

But he understood anyway.

It was the first time in his life that somebody had turned towards his voice, instead of running away. Somebody who had every reason in the world to be afraid. Somebody who was made on purpose to run away from him.

He had turned.

The fireflies carried the cricket over the whole meadow: over the brook, over the oak, over the hedge. From deep in the grass a crooked song rose up, sung by small voices. And then, little by little, they carried him back down, to his root.

Ugo stayed up there for a while, forgetting to hunt.

Nothing else happened that night. Nothing that Ugo knew of, anyway.

The next evening, though, the meadow didn't light up.

The sun went down, the dark came, and the fireflies stayed in the grass, unlit, every one of them. Up at the top of the hill Breath's window went dark as always, and waited. But the meadow didn't answer.

Ugo flew over the dark without understanding. It wasn't the fear of the night Breath came down the hill: it was something else. A heavy silence, like when somebody has gone away from a house.

Then, from deep in the grass, came a chirr.

Just one. Crooked. Wrong.

Then another. Then three. Then twenty, all together, each one wrong in its own way.

Ugo recognised it at once. It was the song of the cricket from the root. The same one from all those years ago, when he used to sing it himself. Only now the fireflies were singing it, and it wasn't beautiful the way it had been: it was crooked. But it was there.

And one by one, the fireflies lit up. And the meadow was a town again.

Ugo never knew what had happened under the root. He only knew that from that evening on, he never heard anyone breathing there again. And that the song, on the other hand, never stopped.

And every time he heard it, that crooked song, he remembered an antenna turned towards him. Just once.

In the dark, sometimes a voice is all you need to know where to go.

Ugo knew now: sometimes you need even less. Sometimes all it takes is for somebody to turn.

Sometimes all it takes is for somebody to turn towards you, my love. Just once.

They don't need to say anything. They don't need to understand everything. It's enough that, instead of going away, they turn to see who you are.

And if one day it's your turn to turn towards somebody, do it. You don't know how much it might mean.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

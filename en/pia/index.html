Prologue · 4 minutes to read aloud

Prologue

Pia's stories — to be read before all the others

I told you the firefly's story because I saw it with my own eyes.

The mole's, on trust. The bat's I heard, every night, through the ceiling.

This one I found.

A little piece at a time, on the ground, among the roots of the old oak at the far end of the meadow.

I have to confess something: when I was your age, I lost everything. Marbles, buttons, pencils, the cap of my water bottle, a key I never found out the use of. I lost them mostly in the meadow, and I lost them for good. Or so I thought.

Then one afternoon, among the roots of the oak, on the sunny side, I found my blue marble.

I hadn't lost it there. I'd lost it by the stream, months before, and the stream is a long way from the oak. But it was mine: it had the same little bubble inside, crooked.

Next to the marble there was a tiny button, with a blade of grass threaded through its holes. There was a thimble full of earth, and in the earth a sprout. And there was a scrap of bark with something written on it, in writing so small that it took me a week, with a magnifying glass, to read it.

It said: You'll find everything here. Sooner or later.

I didn't tell anybody. I already knew what they'd say: that it was the ants, that it was the wind, that I should go and play.

But I started leaving things there, on purpose. A button. A bead. A stub of pencil.

The next morning they were gone.

And in their place, nearly always, there was an acorn. Just one, set down neatly, like the change they give you in a shop.

I kept that up for years. I still have the acorns, in a tin box.

I never saw who lived down there. What I'm going to tell you I put together from what I found, the way you do with a jigsaw when the box is long gone. Some pieces I had to imagine. But the picture, I promise you, is the right one.

Before we start, just one thing.

If you lose something tonight, don't worry too much.

Maybe it isn't lost. Maybe it's just rolling.

This is her story.

It begins like this:

Among the roots of the old oak, at the far end of the meadow, there was a shop where you could find everything.

I · 9 minutes to read aloud

The crooked shop

A bedtime story — Book one

Among the roots of the old oak, at the far end of the meadow, there was a shop where you could find everything.

You didn't see it straight away. You had to know where to look: on the sunny side, where two thick roots cross like folded arms, there was a little round door. And above the little door, a piece of bark with a sign on it, written very small:

Pia's Shop. You'll find everything here. Sooner or later.

Inside, there really was everything.

Buttons of every colour, strung on a blade of grass like a necklace. Thimbles. Bottle tops. Half walnut shells, used as bowls. Beads, smooth pebbles, a spring, tiny keys that opened nothing, a dice with the six worn away. And acorns. Acorns everywhere: in the drawers, under the chairs, inside the boots. There were boots, too, but nobody knew whose.

And the floor was crooked.

It hadn't always been. When Pia had opened the shop, many years before, the floor was as flat as a table. But the oak was alive, and living things grow. A little every year, the roots had swelled, and turned, and shifted, and taken the shop along with them.

Now, if you put a marble down by the door, the marble set off by itself. It rolled under the counter, round the stove, between the legs of the chair, and it always ended up in the same place: the corner at the back, the lowest one.

Pia called it the crooked corner.

Everything, sooner or later, ended up there. That was why Pia never looked for anything. When she needed something, she went to the crooked corner and waited.

Pia was small, with hazelnut fur and two quick black eyes. Her snout was a bit long and her tail was a bit odd: not bushy and plumed like a squirrel's, and not bare and thin like a rat's either. A halfway tail, with a tuft at the end, like a paintbrush.

Where Pia came from, nobody knew. Not even Pia.

The squirrels said her snout was too long for her to be one of them. The rats said her tail was too lovely for her to be one of them. And in the end, after a great deal of arguing, the whole meadow agreed: Pia was a squirrat. Half squirrel, half rat.

Nobody had ever seen another one. But nobody was all that surprised.

Nobody really knows what rats get up to these days.

The shop opened at sunset: the hour when squirrels go to bed and mice wake up. The only hour of the day that belonged a little to both.

That evening, the little door opened just a crack, and in peeped a small face with two pointed ears and a tuft on top.

It was a squirrel. A very young one, with a tail bigger than the rest of him.

“Are you open?” he asked.

“It's always open, when the door opens,” said Pia, from behind the counter. “Come in, you're letting the draught in.”

The squirrel took one step, and the crooked floor made him take three more. He grabbed hold of the counter.

“My mum says I'm not supposed to come here,” he said.

“All mums say that,” said Pia. “What's your name?”

“Tito.”

“And what do you need, Tito?”

Tito looked down at his paws.

“It's my mum's birthday tomorrow. And I haven't got anything to give her. I wanted the most beautiful thing there is.”

Pia thought about it.

“In here,” she said, “everything is the most beautiful thing there is. To somebody.”

“And to my mum?”

“That's for you to know. Come along.”

And she took him to the crooked corner.

It took quite a while. Tito looked at everything: the buttons, the beads, the spring, the dice with the six worn away. He picked a thing up, turned it round, put it down again. Pia said nothing. She stood next to him with her tail curled round, and waited, because in Pia's shop nobody ever chose in a hurry.

Then, on the other side of the shop, something moved.

It rolled out from under a drawer. It went under the counter, round the stove, slipped between the legs of the chair, and stopped, tic, right in front of Tito's paws.

It was a glass bead. Blue like the sky just before it gets dark, with a tiny bubble of air inside.

Tito didn't say a word. He picked it up with both paws and held it against his chest.

“How much is it?” he asked at last, quietly.

There were no prices in Pia's shop. Everybody paid with whatever they had.

Tito rummaged in his fur and pulled out an acorn. Just one, nice and smooth, with its little cap still on. It was the one he'd been saving for the winter: you could tell from the way he looked at it.

Pia took it very seriously.

“It's a deal,” she said.

At the door, Tito turned round.

“Pia,” he said. “My mum says you're half rat.”

“And the other half?”

Tito thought about it.

“She didn't say.”

“There you are,” said Pia. “I don't know either. Happy birthday to your mum.”

When Tito had gone, Pia went out of the little door with the acorn in her paw. She dug a hole among the roots, put the acorn in, covered it up and patted the earth down with her paws, nice and firm. The way squirrels do.

And the way squirrels do, the next morning she couldn't remember where.

Never mind, she thought. Things come back here, sooner or later.

That night, before going to sleep, Pia did what she did every night.

She went to the crooked corner and looked.

The whole meadow, sooner or later, had found what it was looking for there. Not Pia. Because the thing she was looking for had never rolled in: another one like her. She didn't even know what it would look like. But she was sure that, if it ever came, she'd know it straight away.

That night the crooked corner was full of things. But not that one.

Pia sighed, not sadly at all. Then she climbed into her hammock, which was a sock hung between two roots. It was crooked too, so Pia slept with her head a little lower than her feet.

She'd been used to it for a long time.

Some things you don't find by looking for them, my love.

You find them by being in the right place, and waiting, while the world, very slowly, tilts a little towards you.

And now close your eyes. The shop is shut, the crooked corner is waiting, and tomorrow something will roll in.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

The summer of the rats

A bedtime story — Book two, sequel to “The crooked shop”

One rainy evening, something rolled into the crooked corner of Pia's shop that nobody had ever seen before.

It was round and flat, made of yellow metal, with a hole in the middle and letters all the way round. It was heavier than a button and lighter than an acorn, and when it fell on the floor it went tin.

Pia turned it over and over. She didn't know what it was.

But that evening, sheltering from the rain, there was Gaspare.

Gaspare was a toad. He was as old as the stones in the stream and roughly the same shape, and he came to the shop mostly to keep dry, because he said that water, at his age, was best looked at from the outside.

When he saw the yellow thing, Gaspare stopped chewing.

“That,” he said, “is a token. City stuff.”

“City?” said Pia.

The city was down the road, a few hours' walk for anyone with short legs. At night, from the meadow, all you could see of it was an orange glow behind the hill, like a sunset that never ended.

“And how did a city token get all the way here?”

Gaspare settled himself on his cork.

“The rats brought it,” he said. “The summer they came.”

And since it was raining outside and nobody was in a hurry, he told her.

A long time ago, he said, there were no rats in the meadow. There were squirrels in the oak, field mice in the grass, moles underneath, fireflies on top. And that was all.

The rats lived in the city, under the old covered market. They were happy there: there was always something to eat, there were warm pipes in winter and cool cellars in summer, and at night, when the market shut, the whole place was theirs.

Then one day the humans closed the market.

They closed it for good, emptied it, and started knocking it down, with machines that made the ground shake all the way down to the cellars. And the rats had to leave. All of them: the old ones, the little ones, the ones with families and the ones without.

They reached the meadow one summer evening, at sunset, in a line along the edge of the road. Each of them was carrying whatever they had managed to save: one a button, one a bottle top, one a key, one a yellow token with a hole in the middle.

“And how did the meadow welcome them?” asked Pia.

Gaspare thought about it for a moment.

“Badly,” he said. “At first, badly.”

Because the rats were different.

They talked fast, swallowing the ends of their words, with an accent nobody in the meadow had ever heard. They called everybody chief. They said dunno instead of I don't know. And above all, they kept all the wrong hours.

They worked all night long. They dug, and carried, and mended, and counted their stores, and made a terrible racket just when the squirrels were trying to sleep.

And in the daytime, when the squirrels woke up, the rats had finished: so they had a party. You could hear laughing and singing from their holes at midday, as if it were nothing.

“They don't do a thing all day,” said the squirrels.

“They sleep all night,” said the rats, about the squirrels. “Honestly.”

“And really?” asked Pia.

“Really,” said Gaspare, “they all wanted the same things. Somewhere dry. Something put by for the winter. The little ones safe. They just wanted them at different times.”

It took a while.

It took the first big rainstorm of September, when the stream burst its banks and flooded the lowest squirrel holes. It happened at night, and the squirrels were asleep. But the rats were awake.

They were the first to hear the water. They were the ones who woke everybody up, and who dug, in a single night, a channel that carried the water away from the roots, because in the city, with all those pipes, they'd had plenty of practice.

The next morning the squirrels came down from the oak and saw the channel. And they understood something: that having somebody awake at night, while you're asleep, is a stroke of luck.

After that, things changed.

The rats taught the meadow how to swap things: a button for two hazelnuts, a bottle top for a bit of cheese. Nobody in the meadow had ever done that before. The squirrels taught the rats which berries you can eat and which you can't, and where the winter wind blows least.

And since one lot went to bed just as the other lot woke up, they got into the habit of meeting at sunset, among the roots of the old oak. The only hour of the day that belonged a little to both.

Pia's ears went up.

But she didn't say anything.

“And then?” she asked.

“And then,” said Gaspare, “one evening they left.”

In the city they had built a new market, bigger than the old one, with cellars so lovely that word of them reached all the way to the meadow. And the rats were city folk: the meadow was fond of them, but they missed the city.

They left one summer evening, at sunset, in a line along the edge of the road. The way they had come.

They left behind a channel, a great many buttons and bottle tops, and the habit of swapping things. And for a while the meadow was very, very quiet at night.

“Did they really all go?” asked Pia.

Gaspare didn't answer straight away.

“Nearly all,” he said.

“Nearly?”

“There was a squirrel,” said Gaspare. “A young one, with the loveliest tail in the oak. And there was a rat who talked fast and called her chief. They met at sunset, among the roots, every evening. And when the rats left, he was at the very back of the line. The last one.”

“And then?”

Gaspare looked out at the rain.

“Then I don't know,” he said. “I was a young toad, and young toads have other things on their minds.”

Then he looked at Pia. He looked at her slightly long snout, and her halfway tail with the tuft at the end. He looked at her for a very long time, and said nothing.

When the rain stopped, Gaspare went back to the stream.

Pia stayed in the shop on her own. She took the yellow token, threaded a blade of grass through the hole, and hung it round her neck.

She didn't know why. It just seemed like the thing to do.

People who come from far away, my love, sometimes only seem strange because they're awake while we're asleep.

You just have to find the hour that belongs a little to both.

Oh, and one more thing. Scientists say that a squirrel and a rat can't possibly, in any way at all, have babies together.

Scientists are serious people, and they're nearly always right.

But at night, in the meadow, there's never anybody checking.

And now close your eyes. The rain has stopped, the shop is shut, and Pia is asleep, crooked, with a token round her neck.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

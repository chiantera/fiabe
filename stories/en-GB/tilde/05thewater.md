# The water

*A bedtime story — Book Five, following "The neighbour upstairs"*

---

It started with nothing at all.

Tilde was doing the upper round one afternoon in June, and when she reached the west corridor she put a paw down and drew it back.

It was damp.

Not wet: damp. Like a cellar wall.

Tilde stood there for quite a while.

That corridor had been dry for twenty years. She had dug it herself in good pale earth that didn't hold water, and in twenty years nothing had ever happened there, not even after the big storms.

She ran her paw along the whole wall. Damp for four paces, then dry.

*All right*, she thought. *A root has come through.*

The following week it was damp for ten paces.

Tilde stopped doing the upper round the way she always did and began doing it another way: slowly, nose to the floor, stopping now and then to lick a finger and touch the wall and feel where the cold was pulling.

And she found that the damp wasn't a spot. It was a stripe. It came from the north — from the hill side — and ran down towards the brook across half the meadow, like the trail of a snail a hundred metres long.

And it was moving.

Every week it was a little further over.

Tilde didn't know what it was, but she knew perfectly well what it meant: the water under the meadow had changed course.

So she went up to say so.

Up above it was the finest summer anybody could remember.

The grass was tall, greener than it had ever been, full of flowers even in the places where there were usually hardly any. The ants said it was a year to tell your grandchildren about. The snails were fat and happy.

«The water is moving,» said Tilde.

«The water?» said the ants. «It hasn't rained for three weeks.»

«Underneath.»

The ants looked at one another.

«Tilde,» said one of them kindly, «look at this grass.»

And it was true, and that was exactly the problem: the grass was beautiful **because** the water was moving there. The meadow was drinking somebody else's luck without knowing it.

But that was the part Tilde couldn't get across — or rather: she said it, and nobody understood.

«And then what happens?» asked a snail.

«I don't know yet.»

«Then let's wait and see,» said the snail, very reasonably.

Tilde went back down.

Pino found her sitting in the west corridor, still, with her nose against the damp wall.

«What are you doing?»

«Listening.»

Pino put his ear to it as well. «I can't hear anything.»

«Exactly.»

«What am I supposed to hear?»

«Behind there, ten paces along, is the seam of water that used to run under the willow.» Tilde tapped twice with her paw. «I've heard it since I was born. It's the background noise of this house.»

Pino listened harder.

«It's gone?»

«It's further away,» said Tilde. «By quite a bit.»

They were quiet a moment.

«But if everything's fine up above,» said Pino, «maybe it isn't serious.»

«Maybe,» said Tilde.

And that night, for the first time in her life, she went to sleep in a different room: the highest one, nearest the way out.

She didn't tell anybody, and she didn't explain it to herself either. It simply seemed like a good idea.

There are times when you notice something before everybody else, my love.

And when that happens hardly anyone believes you, because they look out of the window and outside there's sunshine.

It doesn't mean you're wrong. It only means you were looking from somewhere else, and from there you see it sooner.

Tell them anyway. And in the meantime, if it feels right, sleep wherever you sleep best.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow everything is quiet — almost everything — the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

# The city that came before

*A bedtime story — Book Ten, following "Digging forwards"*

---

Tilde went back to the half-dug corridor towards the hedge in September.

Not out of restlessness. The new house was straight and it did the job, but the larder was too close to the bed, and a larder that's warm in winter keeps nothing at all. She needed cold dry earth, and the cold dry earth was over that way, under the hedge, where she had never been.

So she dug west, one mole a day, properly.

And after a while the earth began to change.

First it went sandy, which is good news: sand dries. Then it went pale. Then it started smelling of nothing at all.

That unsettles a mole. Earth always tells you something — water came through here, a leaf died here, there's an ants' nest above this bit. That earth told her nothing, like a room nobody has walked into for a very long time.

Tilde slowed down.

Then she pushed her nose forward, and her nose didn't find earth.

It found air.

She came through with the rest of her and dropped about two moles' worth, which for an old mole is quite a lot, and lay on her back for a moment wondering whether she had broken anything.

She hadn't broken anything.

She got up, and she noticed two things straight away.

The first: she was in a corridor.

The second: she hadn't dug it.

It was wide. Wide enough for three abreast. The ceiling was a perfect round curve, the kind you only get if you know what you're doing. And the walls were smooth — not smooth the way a mole makes them on purpose: smooth the way the long corridor in your own house goes after forty years of walking down it.

But nobody had walked down this one in a very long time. You could tell.

Tilde put a paw on her chest and waited to stop being frightened.

Her head, these days, played the odd trick on her — a name that wouldn't come, a thought left half finished — but her legs never did. Her legs never forgot a single thing, and even in a brand new place like this one, they knew exactly where to put themselves.

Then, being a mole, she went to have a look.

The corridor went on. Then it opened into a four-way junction, well made, with the corners rounded off, because sharp corners wear away and fall in.

Off the junction were the rooms.

There was a deep larder, right down low, in the coldest spot, exactly where it belongs.

There was a chamber with a low round ceiling for holding the warmth in.

There was a slope going up with a right-angled bend in it, and Tilde stood looking at that for quite a while, because a bend like that is what stops water, and nobody had ever taught her that: she had worked it out herself at fifty, and she had been very pleased with herself about it.

And there was the place where the corridor went round a big stone instead of through it.

Tilde laughed quietly, on her own, underground.

She went on for an hour, and it didn't end. It was a city. Streets, junctions, rooms, exits plugged up for the winter and never opened again.

There was nobody there.

Not a sound, not a bone, not a smell: only the work, left behind.

Moles, a great many, a very long time ago. Who they were, how many, what they were called — there was no way of knowing, and there never would be.

Tilde sat down in the middle of the four-way junction and said, out loud, to nobody:

«You did a lovely job.»

The next day she brought Pino, who ran up and down for twenty minutes going *oh*, and then asked: «But who were they?»

«I don't know.»

«And what were their names?»

«I don't know, Pino. We'll never know.»

«So what do we do about it?»

Tilde looked at the junction, and the cold larder, and the right-angled bend got exactly right by somebody who died before she was born.

«We live in it», she said.

It took her a fortnight to join her tunnel to theirs, because that particular stretch she wanted done properly.

And Tilde's house was big again. Bigger than the old one, with the larder where a larder goes and two real ways out and a new straight piece, dug by her, joined onto an ancient piece dug by somebody else.

There was one thing she left alone.

At the end of a side branch there was a little room, round, badly made: the curve came out crooked, and you could see exactly where whoever was digging it had gone wrong and then tried to fix it.

It was somebody's first room.

Tilde left it crooked, just as it was.

You're not the first one through here, my love.

Before you there were a great many people you'll never meet, who knew nothing whatever about you: and they dug well, and they left the bends in the right places, and the streets wide, and a cold room for keeping things in.

And now you walk about in it without even noticing.

And one day it'll be your turn to leave something for somebody you'll never meet: dig it straight, please. They'll never say thank you, but they'll need it.

And now close your eyes.

Under the meadow almost everything is quiet, almost all the roads are where they should be, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

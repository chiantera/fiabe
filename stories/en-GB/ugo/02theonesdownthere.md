# The ones down there

*A bedtime story — Book Two, following "The bat nobody could hear"*

---

For a whole summer, the house at the top of the hill belonged to Ugo alone.

He knew it beam by beam. He knew which tile creaked in the wind, where the afternoon warmth collected, and in which corner his voice came back roundest. An empty house answers loudly: every room sends the whole shout back to you, as if it couldn't wait to talk to somebody.

Then, one morning at the end of summer, while Ugo was asleep upside down, the house filled with noise.

Doors. Boxes. Footsteps up and down the stairs. Something enormously heavy dragged across the floor, and somebody saying *careful, careful, careful*.

Ugo opened one eye, listened, and shut it again. In the daytime a bat doesn't bother with anything.

But in the evening, when he went out, the house answered him differently.

It answered softly. The curtains, the beds, the coats hanging behind the door each took a little piece of his voice, and what came back was gentler, and shorter. That's how a full house answers: like somebody with a lot to do who listens to you anyway.

The ones down there had arrived.

Ugo hardly ever saw them. In the daytime he slept; at night they were inside and he was out. But he heard them through the ceiling, and since he didn't know their names, he called them by their sounds.

There was Heavy Step, who went up the stairs two at a time and made the beams shake.

There was Teaspoon: every evening, at the same time, from the kitchen there came a *tink tink tink* of a teaspoon stirring sugar in a cup. Always three times round. Never four.

And there was the smallest sound of all.

It came from the room right under Ugo's beam. It was breathing. Short and quick: somebody small, awake while everyone else was asleep.

Ugo called them Breath.

Breath had a lamp, and kept it on all night. Ugo knew because every evening, as he came out of the hole under the tiles, he saw the window just below: a yellow square, the only lit one on the whole hill.

And the first evening he flew past it, Ugo discovered something wonderful.

The lamp called the mosquitoes.

They came from all over the meadow, drawn by the light and by the warm breath coming through the half-open window. They whined against the glass, looked for a gap, queued up to get in.

Ugo had never seen — or rather, never *heard* — so many mosquitoes in one place.

From that evening on, Breath's window became his favourite place to hunt. He went back and forth in front of the glass, slowly, precisely, and every mosquito that arrived with the idea of getting in changed its mind for good.

Not one mosquito bit Breath, all summer long. Not a single one.

One evening, through the ceiling, Ugo heard Heavy Step say to Teaspoon: «Funny, though. Not one mosquito up here.»

«Must be the hill air,» said Teaspoon. *Tink tink tink.*

Ugo, out there with his mouth full, said nothing. Nobody would have heard him anyway.

Then, at dawn, he went back in through the hole, hung from his beam, and listened.

Underneath him, Breath was still awake. Still short, still quick. The lamp still on.

Ugo knew how to send mosquitoes away. But not that short breathing. He didn't know how yet.

Somebody takes care of you without you knowing, my love.

While you sleep there's somebody closing a window, somebody tucking in a blanket, somebody sending away a thing that whines. You don't see it, and the next day nobody tells you.

You don't need to say thank you. You only need to sleep. That's just fine.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

# The lamp goes out

*A bedtime story — Book Seven, following "The world upside down"*

---

Ugo had always known the fireflies.

He met them every summer night, above the meadow: hundreds of little lights coming on and going off slowly, as if they were breathing. He could hear them with his echo too, small and soft, and he could see them with his eyes, because bats can see perfectly well, whatever people say.

But he never ate them. Never.

Fireflies taste dreadful: Ugo had learnt that when he was young, just once, and once was enough. Ever since, to him, that light was a sign. It meant: *not for you*. And he flew past them, wide and polite, the way you walk past a garden with a sign saying *keep off the grass*.

Apart from that, the fireflies stayed down in the meadow and he stayed up above. They had never said anything to each other. They wouldn't have heard him anyway.

One summer evening Ugo was in his spot: in front of Breath's window, hunting. The lamp was on, as always. The mosquitoes were queuing against the glass, as always.

And from the far end of the meadow a little light came up.

Just one. It flew slowly, a bit crooked, like somebody who has come a long way and never made that journey before. It crossed the brook, crossed the hedge, climbed up over the tall grass. And it landed on the glass.

Ugo swerved wide, as always. *Not for you.*

But as he passed, his echo told him everything. He heard the firefly clinging to the glass, tiny, its wings trembling with the effort. He heard, beyond the glass, the lamp, warm. And he heard Breath, in bed, with the covers pulled up to their nose. Short and quick, as always.

Then the little light on the glass came on. Not bright: soft and warm, like a breath of its own.

And Breath, in there, changed.

It stopped. Then it started again, but slower. Ugo knew Breath well enough to understand what that meant: Breath was looking at something, and wasn't afraid.

It stayed like that for a very long time.

Then, from inside the room, a small sound.

*Click.*

The lamp went out.

For the first time since the house had existed, Breath's window was dark.

And at the bottom of the hill, all at once, the meadow lit up.

Ugo had never seen anything like it. Two hundred little lights appeared in the grass in a single moment, like a town seen from above. The brook began to shine. The oak stood out black and round against everything else. They had been there every evening, and he had flown over them a thousand times: but next to that lit window, from up high, you could hardly see them.

That night, coming back under the tiles at dawn, Ugo heard Breath sleeping.

And the breathing was longer than it had ever been.

It was only the next day, at suppertime, that Ugo understood what all this cost.

The window was dark that evening too. And with no lamp, the mosquitoes didn't come to the window any more.

His favourite spot was finished.

For a few evenings he went back anyway, out of habit: to and fro in front of the dark glass, waiting for a queue that never came. Then he gave up, and went hunting over the brook.

It was further away. It was colder. The mosquitoes were scattered about, and he had to chase them one by one instead of finding them all in a row, waiting. Ugo came home in the mornings more tired, and sometimes still a bit hungry.

And yet he was happy.

He couldn't quite explain why. He only knew that every evening, as he came out of the hole, the window below went dark and the meadow down there lit up, as if they were saying goodnight to each other. And he knew that underneath him, now, somebody was really sleeping.

He had lost his favourite spot. And in exchange he lived above a ceiling that people slept well under.

It seemed like a good bargain to him.

That same summer something else happened too, and Ugo never understood it.

Some nights, flying low along the walls of the house, he heard a deep scraping, under the ground. Something digging slowly, round the foundations, a little at a time, stopping often, like somebody tired or somebody with old legs.

Ugo dropped right down to the grass and called.

And only his own voice came back.

The scraping went on for a few nights. Then it moved off towards the meadow, and wasn't heard again.

Ugo never found out who it was. Sometimes, still, he wondered.

Sometimes somebody else's good news costs you something, my love.

Somebody finds their courage, and you lose a spot. Somebody is happy, and you have to go the long way round.

It doesn't mean the news is bad. It only means that sometimes lovely things have a small price, and that sometimes you're the one who pays it.

And that you can be happy anyway.

And now close your eyes.

Above the meadow everything is quiet, under the roof somebody is breathing, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

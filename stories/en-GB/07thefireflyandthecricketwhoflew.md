# The firefly and the cricket who flew

*A bedtime story — Book Seven, following "The firefly and whoever came down the hill"*

---

Another September came, and Rocco had grown very old.

He hardly ever came out of his little house under the root of the oak. His legs didn't hold him well, and the walk down to the brook, which he used to do in a single night, was too far now.

So it was Nina who went to him.

She went every evening, as soon as the sun disappeared behind the trees. She landed on the root, put out her lantern and sat there.

She did nothing: that was exactly the point. He had taught her that, years before, on a cold stone beside the water.

They talked all the same, in their own way. Nina asked, and Rocco answered with his antennae: up meant yes, down meant no.

«Are you cold?»

Down.

«Shall I call Bea?»

Down.

«Do you want anything?»

And that evening, for the first time in a long while, the antennae went up.

Nina sat up.

«Tell me.»

Rocco slowly lifted one leg, and pointed upwards.

«The hill?» said Nina. «Do you want to see the window?»

Down.

Rocco insisted, and raised his leg a little higher still. Not the hill. Not the window.

*Above.*

And then Nina understood, and felt something tighten in her chest.

Rocco wanted to see the meadow.

The meadow he was born in, the one he had sung in every night of every summer of his life, and had never once seen — because crickets live in the grass, and from the grass all you can see is more grass.

«Wait here,» said Nina, which was a slightly silly thing to say. «Don't move.»

She came back with Bea. Bea came back with the others. And they came back with all of them.

It took a great many. An old cricket weighs almost nothing, but a firefly weighs even less, and it took forty of them: under his legs, under his belly, at the sides, holding him gently the way you hold a thing that can break.

«Rocco,» said Nina. «Don't be afraid.»

And they lifted him.

They passed the blades of grass, which from below looked like a forest and from above were only grass. They passed the flowers closed up for the night. And then there was nothing above them at all, only air, and the meadow opened out all at once below.

Rocco looked.

He saw the brook, making a silver curve and disappearing behind the hedge.

He saw the old oak from outside, black and enormous, and in among the roots that little dot of dry earth that was his house.

He saw the flat stone where Nina had gone out, one September night many years before.

He saw, far away at the top of the hill, a yellow square window.

And he saw the fireflies: two hundred of them, spread across the whole meadow, on and off, on and off, like a city breathing.

Then, from below, came the song.

It was the new fireflies, still down in the grass: twenty thin crooked *frin*s, each wrong in its own way, rising out of the grass all together.

His song.

Rocco listened to it with his antennae straight up, high above the meadow, carried by forty fireflies.

Then they brought him back down, very slowly, to the root.

Nina stayed with him.

The cricket turned his head and looked at her, and said nothing, because he hadn't been able to say anything for three summers.

«I know,» said Nina.

That night Rocco fell asleep under his root, the way he did every night.

And in the morning he didn't wake up.

The following evening, in the meadow at the foot of the hill, nobody lit up.

The dark came down, and the meadow stayed dark. The fireflies sat in the grass, still, with their lanterns out, and not one of them felt like switching hers on.

Up at the top of the hill, at a certain point, a yellow window went dark. And somebody stayed waiting behind the glass, the way they did every evening.

But the meadow didn't light up.

Then, from deep in the grass, came a *frin*.

It was Bea. On her own, crooked, wrong as always.

Then another. Then three. Then twenty all together, each wrong in its own way, and Rocco's song filled the meadow from one side to the other.

And then, one at a time, the fireflies lit up.

Nina first. Then Bea. Then ten, then a hundred, then the whole meadow, the way a city comes on when evening falls.

And on the other side of the glass, up there, somebody saw.

From that night the song has never stopped, and every evening Nina spends a moment on the root, unlit, doing nothing.

One day somebody who taught you something won't be there any more, my love.

It happens, and it hurts, and there's no way at all of making it not happen.

But you'll still know how to do that thing.

And every time you do it — even if it comes out crooked, even if you get it wrong in your own way — that somebody is there, a little bit.

And now close your eyes. The meadow is singing, the oak is on watch, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

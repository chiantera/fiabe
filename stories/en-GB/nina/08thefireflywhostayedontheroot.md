# The firefly who stayed on the root

*A bedtime story — Book Eight, following "The firefly and the cricket who flew"*

---

The following summer, the meadow at the foot of the hill was fuller of fireflies than it had ever been.

And the one everybody called was Bea.

She had become the fastest of all of them, and her green light could be seen from one end of the meadow to the other. She lit the path for the ants coming home late. She made light for the old mole who could never find her own front door. She saw home the lost snails, the muddled moths, the dandelion seeds that had lost the wind.

Nina, meanwhile, had grown old.

She hadn't noticed it all at once. She had noticed it a piece at a time: first the brook seeming further away, then the hedge becoming hard work, then certain evenings when she lit up and the light came on slowly, like a thing that has to think about it for a moment.

And so, almost without deciding to, she had got into the habit of staying on the root of the oak.

At first she went because she missed Rocco.

Then she went anyway, because it had become her place.

She sat there every evening, unlit, and watched the meadow work.

And watching, one evening, she saw something she recognised.

She saw Bea cross the meadow six times in an hour. She saw her never stop. She saw her light up harder when she was tired, so as to manage all the same.

And Nina said nothing.

She didn't say *stop*, she didn't say *rest*, she didn't say *watch me*. Because there was something she had learned many years before, under a clover leaf, and it had cost her dearly: that certain things can't be said to somebody. All you can do is sit there and wait for them to arrive on their own.

She waited all summer.

Then September came.

The nights drew out and turned cold. The brook slowed down. The flowers closed up and didn't open again.

And one night, in the middle of the meadow, Bea's light went out.

Not slowly, the way it does when a firefly breathes. It just went out, like a candle in the wind.

Nina saw it from the root. And she knew at once where she had ended up, because she knew: there's a flat stone by the brook where you end up when that happens.

It took her an age to get there.

Once it had been a moment's flight. That night it was quite another matter: she stopped twice in the grass to get her breath, and when she finally landed on the stone she was puffing, because it was quite a journey for an old firefly.

Bea was there, grey, with her face turned away.

«Go away,» she said. «I'm out.»

Nina sat down next to her.

«I saw you from up there,» she said.

«But how? I'm not making any light.»

And then Nina said something that wasn't hers. She said it quietly, and as she said it she felt a kind of warmth in her chest, because it was the first time she had been the one saying it.

«I never looked for your light, Bea. I looked for *you*.»

Bea turned round.

«Your lantern isn't broken,» Nina went on. «It's tired. You've lit this whole meadow all summer long and you haven't stopped for a single night.»

«But if I stop, the dark comes back.»

«The dark comes back anyway,» said Nina. «It comes back every evening. It isn't your fault and it isn't your job. You're a firefly, not the sun.»

Bea sat quietly for a long time, listening to the water.

«And if someone gets lost?» she said at last.

And that was the question a cricket had once answered.

Nina didn't answer. She lifted her antennae towards the grass.

And out of the grass, softly, came a *frin*. And another. And then twenty together, crooked and thin and wrong each in its own way.

«There,» said Nina. «They'll see to it.»

Bea cried a little, the sort of crying that comes when somebody can finally stop.

And then, for the first time in months, she didn't fly.

She stayed on the stone, beside her friend, and together they looked at the September sky, which was full, full, full of stars.

Bea slept all autumn and all winter, in the warm heart of the old oak.

And in spring she lit up by herself, with no effort, brighter than before.

Nina watched her light up from the root, unlit, doing nothing.

And she didn't even feel like flying.

One day you'll say a sentence to somebody, my love, and halfway through you'll realise the sentence isn't yours.

It belongs to somebody who said it to you when you needed it, a long time ago.

Never mind: it's yours as well now. It works just the same. It works better.

That's how people stay, once they're gone.

And now close your eyes. The meadow is singing, the old firefly is on watch, and tomorrow there will be light again.

Goodnight. 🌙✨

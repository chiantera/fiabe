# Epilogue

*Nina's stories — to be read after all the others*

---

There. It's finished.

Ten nights, one at a time, and now you know the whole story of the firefly.

And I know exactly what you're about to ask me, because I used to ask it too at your age, every time somebody finished telling me something.

*But is it true?*

All right, let's do this: I'll tell you what's true, piece by piece, and then you can decide.

The meadow is true. It's still down there, at the foot of the hill, and if we go tomorrow morning you'll see it: an ordinary meadow, with long grass, a brook that makes a curve, a hedge and a big oak at the far end.

The oak is true. So are the roots, and under one root, on the shady side, there really is a little corner of dry earth.

The fireflies are true. They make light for real, no tricks, and when there are a lot of them the grass looks as though it's lit from underneath.

The crickets are true, and they really do sing like that: if one summer night you're quiet for long enough, you'll hear them all stop together and then start again.

The dark is true. And being afraid of the dark is the truest thing of all.

Nina, on the other hand, is the name I gave her.

Because that night a firefly really did settle on the glass of my window, while I couldn't sleep. And I really did turn off the lamp. And what I saw afterwards was so big that it wouldn't fit into one sentence, and so it took me ten nights.

That's what stories are for: to hold on to something you've seen, when it's too big to hold in your hands.

Now sit up a moment. Come here.

No, don't turn anything on.

Kneel up on the bed, face against the glass, hands round your eyes to make a curtain — like that, the way I used to.

And now wait.

I know you can't see anything. At the start you never can: eyes take a while to stop remembering the light.

You have to keep still. Which is the hardest thing in the world, as we know.

Wait just a little longer.

There.

Now, down at the bottom.

Can you see them?

Nina and Rocco aren't there any more, and neither is Bea, because a great deal of time has passed. Those are their granddaughters, and their granddaughters' granddaughters, and they know nothing at all about the two of us watching them through the glass.

But the song you can hear — the crooked thin one, that everybody gets wrong in their own way — is still his.

Look as long as you like. They won't run off.

And when you're grown up, if you ever get the chance, take somebody to a window in the dark yourself. There's no need to explain anything. You just turn off the light and stay there beside them, until it comes on by itself.

Now get back under the covers, because it's terribly late and your eyes are closing.

I'll leave the lamp on for you if you want it. That's absolutely fine: nobody down there takes it badly.

*«Will you tell it again tomorrow?»*

Yes, my love.

Tomorrow and every evening.

Goodnight. 🌙✨
